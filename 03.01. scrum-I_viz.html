1.1 · What Scrum is, in one sentence

Last week we worked with the Agile Manifesto: four values and twelve principles. They tell a team what to prefer — working product over documentation, responding to change over following a plan — but they do not tell anyone what to do on Monday morning at nine. Today we look at the most widely used answer to that second question. Its name is Scrum, and its official definition is a single sentence.

“Scrum is a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems.”

— The Scrum Guide 2020, © Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, offered under the CC BY-SA 4.0 (Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0) licence — scrumguides.org

Every word of that sentence carries a precise meaning, so we take them one at a time.

  • Lightweight — Scrum has very few rules. The complete official text, the Scrum Guide, is about thirteen pages long. Compare it with project-management standards of several hundred pages.
  • Framework — a minimal set of mandatory elements that a team completes with its own practices. In Scrum the mandatory elements are three accountabilities, five events, three artifacts and the rules that connect them. Scrum requires, for example, a Daily Scrum of at most 15 minutes; it does not say which questions are asked in it or which board records the answers. The Guide itself says the framework is purposefully incomplete.
  • Compare three words you will meet all semester. A process is a fixed sequence of steps with defined inputs and outputs. A methodology (Class 1) is a complete, shared way of working that sets how a team plans, decides, delivers and learns. A framework is the smallest of the three: it gives the structure and leaves the techniques to the team.
  • Value — the benefit the product creates for the people who use it and for the organization that builds it. For Team Menu's allergen page, value is measured in minutes saved at the counter and in allergic reactions avoided — not in the number of screens built.
  • Adaptive solutions — solutions that are changed as soon as new information shows that they should be.
  • Complex problem — a problem whose requirements, and the means to meet them, are not fully known at the start, so that the result of a decision can only be known after it has been tried. Building a new digital product for real users is almost always complex: nobody knows in advance which features students will actually use.

1.2 · Where Scrum comes from — seven dates

Scrum was not invented in one meeting. It grew in three stages: an observation about manufacturing companies, a first software team, and a written definition that has been revised several times. Write these dates down; the timeline is part of your D3 (Deliverable 3, Scrum Team Charter) and of CQ1 (Checkpoint Quiz 1).

Scrum's history in seven dates. Sources: the linked originals.
YearWhat happenedWhy it matters
1986Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka publish The New New Product Development Game in the HBR (Harvard Business Review). They studied how companies such as Fuji-Xerox, Canon, Honda and NEC developed new copiers, cameras, cars and computers.They contrasted a sequential organization of the work (each group of specialists finishes its phase and hands the result to the next group) with a holistic one (one multidisciplinary team works on the product from start to finish and the phases overlap). They named the second approach after the rugby scrum — the name of the framework comes from this article.
1993Jeff Sutherland's team at Easel Corporation, a software company near Boston that no longer exists, runs a software project in short, fixed cycles with a short daily team meeting (InfoQ, 2006).The first Scrum team in software.
1995Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland present Scrum at the OOPSLA (Object-Oriented Programming, Systems, Languages & Applications) '95 conference in Austin, Texas, run by the ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) (SIGPLAN (Special Interest Group on Programming Languages)). Schwaber's paper, SCRUM Development Process, describes systems development as "an unpredictable, complicated process".First public description of Scrum for software, and the origin of its empirical theory (Lesson 2).
2001Schwaber and Sutherland are two of the seventeen authors of the Manifesto for Agile Software Development (Class 2).Scrum becomes one of the frameworks that put the Manifesto into practice.
2010The two authors publish the first Scrum Guide, the official and free definition of Scrum (revision history).From now on, "Scrum" means what the Guide says. Revisions follow in 2011, 2013, 2016, 2017 and 2020.
2016The Guide adds the five Scrum values: commitment, focus, openness, respect, courage.The values describe the behaviour that makes the framework work (Lesson 2).
2020On November 18, 2020 — 25 years after the 1995 presentation — the current edition appears (press release): shorter, less prescriptive, written for any field.One Scrum Team instead of a team inside a team; Developers; self-managing; a new Product Goal; one commitment per artifact. This is the edition we use.

Why spend three minutes on history in a CS (Computer Science) program? Because the 1986 article studied copiers, cameras and cars — not code. Scrum describes how a group of people organizes complex work, whatever that work is. This is why it is used today for marketing campaigns, hotel renovations and school projects, and why you can use it for a product that requires no programming at all — which was Denning's point in Class 2.

About the name. Scrum is a rugby term. In this course it is only a name: the method is defined by what the 1986 authors measured — one multidisciplinary team, overlapping phases, self-organization, learning across functions — and today by the Scrum Guide, not by any comparison with a sport.

1.3 · The whole framework on one page: 3 · 5 · 3

Everything in Scrum fits on one page — Diagram 1, below. Learn it as three numbers: three accountabilities, five events, three artifacts, and each artifact carries one commitment.

The Scrum framework as 3 · 5 · 3 (Scrum Guide 2020).
ElementWhat it isThe items
3 accountabilitiesThe people, each answerable for one result.Product Owner — the value of the product; Scrum Master — Scrum being understood and used, and the team's effectiveness; Developers — a usable Increment every Sprint.
5 eventsMeetings with a fixed purpose and a time-box (a maximum duration that is never extended).The Sprint (a fixed period of one month or less that contains the other four), Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum (15 minutes), the Sprint Review and the Sprint Retrospective.
3 artifactsThe work made visible, so that it can be inspected.Product Backlog → commitment: Product Goal; Sprint Backlog → commitment: Sprint Goal; Increment → commitment: Definition of Done.

Now read Diagram 1 from left to right. The Product Owner keeps the Product Backlog in order. In Sprint Planning the Scrum Team takes items from its top and writes a Sprint Goal; those items plus the plan to deliver them form the Sprint Backlog. Every day of the Sprint the Developers work and meet for the Daily Scrum. What meets the Definition of Done at the end is the Increment: the team shows it to stakeholders at the Sprint Review, then looks at its own way of working in the Sprint Retrospective — and the next Sprint starts immediately.

One word needs attention. Our syllabus says "roles". The 2020 Guide says accountabilities. A role is a title on a badge; an accountability is a result that one person must answer for. Scrum cares about the second, and so will your role cards in D3 (Deliverable 3, Scrum Team Charter). Next Monday, in Class 4, we open the events and the artifacts in detail. Today we keep the map and focus on the people.

Terms to learn

Scrum
A lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems (Scrum Guide 2020).
Framework
A minimal set of mandatory elements — here accountabilities, events, artifacts and rules — completed by the team's own practices.
Complex problem
A problem whose requirements and means are not fully known in advance; results can only be known after trying.
Scrum Guide
The official, free definition of Scrum by Schwaber and Sutherland; current edition November 2020.
Accountability
A defined result that one person must answer for (the 2020 word for what the syllabus calls a role).
Time-box
A maximum duration for an event, which is never extended.
3 · 5 · 3
3 accountabilities, 5 events, 3 artifacts — each artifact with one commitment.
Commitment
The goal or standard attached to an artifact: Product Goal, Sprint Goal, Definition of Done.

Read-aloud script · 10 minutes

2.1 · Two ways to keep work under control

Engineers distinguish two ways of keeping a process under control, and the choice between them decides almost everything else about how a team works.

  • Defined process control applies when every step is known in advance and the same inputs always produce the same outputs. You can plan the whole process once, repeat it, and check the result at the end. A bottling line works this way; so does compiling the same source code twice — the output is identical every time.
  • Empirical process control applies when the process contains factors you cannot know in advance: what users will actually do, how long an unfamiliar task will take, what the kitchen will change on its menu next week. The outputs vary. You keep such a process under control with a feedback loop: you measure the real result often, compare it with the goal, and correct the process before the next round.

In his 1995 paper Ken Schwaber placed software development in the second family: an "unpredictable, complicated process". Scrum is built on that choice, and the Scrum Guide names it empiricism.

“Empiricism asserts that knowledge comes from experience and making decisions based on what is observed. Lean thinking reduces waste and focuses on the essentials.”

— The Scrum Guide 2020, section "Scrum Theory" (CC BY-SA 4.0 (Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0))

Scrum applies empiricism with the two words you learned in Class 2: it is iterative — the same cycle is repeated — and incremental — each cycle adds a usable piece of product. The length of the loop matters. With a two-week Sprint, at most two weeks of work can rest on a wrong assumption before the Sprint Review exposes it. With a six-month plan, six months can.

Put numbers on it. Suppose an assumption is wrong from the first day — for example, that students read the allergen page at home before leaving for campus, when in fact they read it in the queue. With two-week Sprints, the first Review comes after ten working days: at most ten days of work are built on the error. With a six-month plan tested at the end, about 120 working days are built on it before anyone checks. In Class 2 you saw that the cost of a change grows with the amount of work that depends on it. A short loop keeps that amount small.

2.2 · The three pillars

The Scrum Guide calls the three parts of the empirical loop its pillars. Learn them in this order, because each one makes the next one possible.

The three pillars of empiricism (Scrum Guide 2020), applied to Team Menu.
PillarWhat the Scrum Guide requiresTeam Menu, concretely

The dependency runs in one direction only. Transparency makes inspection possible: the Guide says that inspection without transparency "is misleading and wasteful". Inspection makes adaptation possible: inspection without adaptation is "considered pointless". A team that looks at its errors every Friday but changes nothing has inspection without adaptation.

Where does inspection happen in Scrum? The Guide says that each event is a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt. Next week we open each event in detail; for today, one line each:

  • Sprint Planning inspects the Product Backlog and the team's capacity, and adapts the plan for the coming Sprint.
  • the Daily Scrum inspects progress toward the Sprint Goal every working day, and adapts the Sprint Backlog;
  • the Sprint Review inspects the Increment with the stakeholders, and adapts the Product Backlog;
  • the Sprint Retrospective inspects the team's own way of working, and adapts it for the next Sprint.

Inspection also needs a reference. An acceptable limit is a measurable tolerance agreed in advance. For an allergen page the limit agreed with the cafeteria manager is zero: no wrong entry may reach a student. Without a stated limit, an inspection cannot tell whether what it found is a deviation — a measured result outside the limit — or normal variation.

2.3 · The five values

Pillars are carried out by people, and people need agreed behaviours. Since 2016 the Scrum Guide adds five values. For each one, the right-hand column shows the kind of sentence your team will write in its values pledge for D3 (Deliverable 3, Scrum Team Charter): a behaviour that someone could observe on a given day.

Values and pillars are connected. Openness produces transparency. Courage makes an honest inspection possible — it takes courage to say that three entries out of twenty-four were wrong. Commitment and focus turn the result of an inspection into a real change. Respect is what allows a team to manage itself. The Guide sums it up: when these values are lived by the Scrum Team and the people they work with, the pillars "come to life building trust".

The reverse is just as precise. Without openness, the board shows only part of the work and the inspection looks at an incomplete picture. Without courage, a wrong result is hidden until the end — exactly what Team B does in Example 2. Without focus, the team starts five things and finishes none, so there is nothing usable to inspect at the Review. The values are not decoration: each one keeps one part of the loop working.

Step by step — how the code works

  1. Assumptions in one place. LIMIT = 0, PER_WEEK = 24 and the three LEVELS (1 wrong in 8, in 25, in 100) are the only inputs. Change one, press Restart, and the whole simulation follows.
  2. Deterministic errors. wrongThisWeek() does not use chance. With "1 wrong in N", the entries number N, 2N, 3N… typed since the last process change are wrong. Example: with N = 8, a week of 24 entries contains entries 8, 16 and 24 → 3 wrong. The same run always gives the same numbers, so the class can discuss them.
  3. Monday — transparency. Both teams publish 24 entries. The columns appear faded: the errors have reached students, but no team knows it yet.
  4. Friday — inspection. Team A compares every entry with its recipe card. The column becomes solid and its number appears: the deviation is now known. Its size is compared with LIMIT.
  5. Friday — adaptation. If the inspection found more than the limit, A.level goes up by one (v0 → v1 → v2) and the counter of typed entries restarts. The process box on the right shows the version in use.
  6. Team B. No inspection until week 6; then, in a single step, all 18 wrong entries are revealed at once.
  7. Controls. Start runs one step every 1.4 seconds; Pause keeps the step number, so Start resumes where you stopped; Next step advances one step at a time; Restart rebuilds both teams from zero.
  8. Try it. Set LIMIT = 1 and Restart: Team A no longer adapts after week 3, because one wrong entry is now inside the limit. Then ask: is one wrong allergen entry an acceptable limit?

3.1 · Two accountabilities, two different results

Inside every Scrum Team, two people hold an accountability that nobody else holds. The Product Owner answers for the value of the product. The Scrum Master answers for Scrum being understood and used, and for the effectiveness of the team. Keep that difference in mind: almost every confusion between the two comes from forgetting it.

3.2 · The Product Owner

“The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team.”

— The Scrum Guide 2020 (CC BY-SA 4.0 (Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0))

First, distinguish output from value. Output is the amount produced: screens, features, pages. Value is the effect of that output on the people who use it and on the organization. Ten features that no student opens are a large output and a value of zero. The Product Owner is measured on value.

The Guide lists four parts of Product Backlog management, all under the Product Owner's accountability:

  1. developing and explicitly communicating the Product Goal — the long-term objective of the product, written in the Product Backlog (Team Menu's: every student can check the allergens of every hot meal in the main cafeteria before lining up); the Scrum Team fulfils or abandons one Product Goal before taking on the next;
  2. creating and clearly communicating Product Backlog items;
  3. ordering the Product Backlog items;
  4. ensuring that the Product Backlog is transparent, visible and understood.

Ordering means one sequence — first, second, third — with no ties. The Guide does not impose a formula. The Product Owner weighs four factors: value, size (the effort, estimated by the Developers), risk and dependencies. A simple and common heuristic is value per point: value divided by size. Team Menu's item "3 hot meals with their allergens" has a value of 10 and a size of 3, so 3.3; "Dark mode" has 2 and 2, so 1.0. Dependencies come before the formula: icons that show allergens cannot come before the list of meals they belong to, whatever their ratio.

The Product Owner is one person, not a committee. The cafeteria manager, the students and the kitchen staff are stakeholders: people who are affected by the product or can affect it. They influence the backlog by convincing the Product Owner, and the organization must respect the Product Owner's decisions. The Product Owner may delegate work — Developers often write backlog items — but remains accountable.

An anti-pattern is a practice that looks reasonable but defeats the purpose of the rule it imitates. Four are frequent: the absent Product Owner (the team guesses the priorities), the committee (five people order the backlog and nobody decides), the proxy without authority (every decision waits for someone outside the team) and the Product Owner who tells the Developers how to do the work.

3.3 · The Scrum Master

The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide — helping everyone in the team and in the organization understand the theory and the practice — and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. The Guide adds: Scrum Masters are "true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the larger organization".

Until 2017 the Guide used the word servant-leader, taken from Robert K. Greenleaf's 1970 essay The Servant as Leader (Greenleaf Center for Servant Leadership): leadership whose first priority is the growth and well-being of the people it serves. The 2020 wording keeps that idea and stresses that the Scrum Master is a real leader, not an assistant.

The three groups the Scrum Master serves.
The Scrum Master serves……by (Scrum Guide 2020)
the Scrum Teamcoaching self-management and cross-functionality; helping the team focus on high-value Increments that meet the Definition of Done; causing the removal of impediments; ensuring that every Scrum event takes place and is positive, productive and kept within its time-box.
the Product Ownerhelping find techniques for defining the Product Goal and managing the Product Backlog; helping the team understand the need for clear, concise backlog items; helping establish empirical product planning; facilitating stakeholder collaboration.
the organizationleading, training and coaching its Scrum adoption; planning and advising implementations; helping employees and stakeholders understand and enact an empirical approach; removing barriers between stakeholders and Scrum Teams.

An impediment is an obstacle to the team's progress that the Developers cannot remove by themselves in reasonable time — no access to the kitchen's recipe data, a publishing permission that only the campus web office can give. Read the Guide's verb carefully: the Scrum Master causes the removal. That means making sure the obstacle disappears — by coaching the team to remove it, by bringing the person who can remove it into the conversation, or by acting directly when that is the fastest legitimate way. It does not mean doing every repair personally.

What does that look like on an ordinary day? The Scrum Master watches whether each event produces what it is for — a Sprint Goal at the end of Planning, a re-planned day at the end of the Daily Scrum — notices the blockers that come back week after week, and asks questions more often than giving answers: "What have you tried? Who can decide this? What would let you finish today?" The aim is a team that needs the Scrum Master less every Sprint.

The Scrum Master is not a project manager: no assigning of tasks, no deciding of scope, no reports on individual performance. Four anti-patterns to recognize: the boss (assigns tasks), the secretary (only books rooms and writes minutes), the hero (solves every problem, so the team never learns to solve them) and the absentee.

Read-aloud script · 10 minutes

4.1 · One Scrum Team

“The Scrum Team consists of one Scrum Master, one Product Owner, and Developers. Within a Scrum Team, there are no sub-teams or hierarchies.”

— The Scrum Guide 2020 (CC BY-SA 4.0 (Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0))

The Guide describes the Scrum Team as a cohesive unit of professionals focused on one objective at a time: the Product Goal. One team, one goal, three accountabilities — and no boss inside the team.

4.2 · The Developers

The Developers are "the people in the Scrum Team that are committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint". Read "any aspect" carefully: Developers is not a job title for programmers. The person who checks the allergen data, the one who designs the phone layout, the one who tests it and the one who translates it into French are all Developers when they create the Increment. Team Menu's page can be built with a no-code site builder; nobody has to write code for it to be a real Increment.

The Guide gives the Developers four accountabilities:

  1. creating a plan for the Sprint — the Sprint Backlog;
  2. instilling quality by adhering to a Definition of Done;
  3. adapting their plan each day toward the Sprint Goal;
  4. holding each other accountable as professionals.

In 2020 the Guide replaced "Development Team" by "Developers". The old wording had produced a team inside the team, and with it an "us and them" relation between the Development Team and the Product Owner. Today there is one Scrum Team with three accountabilities.

4.3 · Cross-functional and self-managing

A Scrum Team is cross-functional: its members have, together, all the skills needed to create value in each Sprint. The test applies to the team, not to each person — nobody has to know everything, but the team must not wait for someone outside it to finish the Increment. Team Menu needs six skills every Sprint: user research, allergen data, interface design, page building, testing on phones, and French. Example 4 checks them one by one.

A Scrum Team is also self-managing: it "internally decides who does what, when, and how". Until 2020 the Guide said self-organizing, which covered mainly the how. Self-managing does not mean "no rules": the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal and the Definition of Done set the boundaries; the Product Owner decides the order of value; the Scrum Master coaches. Inside those boundaries, the team decides.

In Team Menu, self-management sounds like this at a Daily Scrum: Karim takes the icons because he built the page template; Sofia pairs with him because she reads French; Noah checks the three new entries against the recipe cards before lunch. Nobody assigned those tasks. And when Karim says on Wednesday that two entries were published without the second check, the team does not wait for a manager: it adds a line to its board policy and checks the two entries that day. That is what the Guide means by holding each other accountable as professionals.

4.4 · Small on purpose: typically 10 or fewer

The Guide asks for a team "small enough to remain nimble and large enough to complete significant work within a Sprint, typically 10 or fewer people". The reason can be computed. Every pair of people in a team is one communication path — one channel that has to be kept up to date. In a team of n people the number of paths is:

paths = n × (n − 1) ÷ 2
The number of people grows linearly; the number of paths grows with the square of n.

From 4 to 10 people the team becomes 2.5 times larger, but the number of paths becomes 7.5 times larger (6 → 45). Doubling a team roughly quadruples its paths. Try one yourself: a team of 8 has 8 × 7 ÷ 2 = 28 paths. Fred Brooks, who received the ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) Turing Award in 1999, described this overhead in The Mythical Man-Month (1975) together with what is now called Brooks's law: "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" — the newcomers must be trained, and every new person adds new paths.

Paths cost time. If every path needs only five minutes a week to stay up to date — a message, a question at the coffee machine — a team of 4 spends 30 minutes a week on it, and a team of 10 spends 225 minutes, almost four hours. The five minutes are an assumption for the calculation; the shape of the result is not.

When a product needs more people, Scrum does not grow the team: it creates several cohesive Scrum Teams, each focused on the same product, sharing the same Product Goal, the same Product Backlog and the same Product Owner.

4.5 · How a student team maps onto Scrum

Your team of four has one Product Owner, one Scrum Master — and everyone builds. The Guide allows it explicitly: "If the Product Owner or Scrum Master are actively working on items in the Sprint Backlog, they participate as Developers."

This course adds one rule of its own: the accountabilities rotate, so that each of you practises each of them. Everyone is Product Owner once and Scrum Master once before the Mid-Term, and the rotation continues in the Design Thinking studio weeks. Be precise when you explain it in D3 (Deliverable 3, Scrum Team Charter): in professional teams the Product Owner and the Scrum Master usually keep their accountability for many Sprints. The rotation is a learning device of this course, not a rule of Scrum.

Step by step — how the code works

  1. Inputs. MEMBERS (4 names), SLOTS (8 Team Studios), BEFORE_MIDTERM = 4, SKILLS (6) and LEVELS (each member's 0–2 rating per skill) are the only data.
  2. Round-robin. assign(i) uses the remainder operator %: Product Owner = MEMBERS[i % 4], Scrum Master = MEMBERS[(i + 1) % 4]. For i = 3 the Scrum Master is MEMBERS[4 % 4] = MEMBERS[0] = Amira — the list wraps around.
  3. Rule check. checkRotation() counts, for each member, the slots before the Mid-Term in which that member is Product Owner and Scrum Master. Both counts must equal 1, and no slot may have the same person twice.
  4. Skills. checkSkill(i) colours one row of the matrix — the darker the cell, the higher the level, and the number is printed too — and writes ✓ when the best level is 2, "gap" otherwise.
  5. Self-management. The decision step does not compute anything: it records what the team itself decided to do about the gap. A self-managing team owns that kind of decision.
  6. Communication paths. paths(n) returns n × (n − 1) ÷ 2. drawPaths() draws one bar per team size from 2 to 15 and emphasizes 4 (Team Menu) and 10 (the Guide's "typically 10 or fewer"). Hover over a bar for its value.
  7. Controls. Start / Pause / Next step / Restart as in the other examples.
  8. Try it. Add a fifth member to MEMBERS and a fifth row to LEVELS, then Restart: with five members and four slots, one member is never Product Owner before the Mid-Term — the check turns ✗, and the team has to change its slots or its rule.

Glossary · every term to learn today

Scrum
A lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems (Scrum Guide 2020). Lesson 1
Framework
A minimal set of mandatory elements — here accountabilities, events, artifacts and rules — completed by the team's own practices. Lesson 1
Complex problem
A problem whose requirements and means are not fully known in advance; results can only be known after trying. Lesson 1
Scrum Guide
The official, free definition of Scrum by Schwaber and Sutherland; current edition November 2020. Lesson 1
Accountability
A defined result that one person must answer for (the 2020 word for what the syllabus calls a role). Lesson 1
Time-box
A maximum duration for an event, which is never extended. Lesson 1
3 · 5 · 3
3 accountabilities, 5 events, 3 artifacts — each artifact with one commitment. Lesson 1
Commitment
The goal or standard attached to an artifact: Product Goal, Sprint Goal, Definition of Done. Lesson 1
Defined process control
Control by planning: known steps, same inputs give the same outputs, checked at the end. Lesson 2
Empirical process control
Control by feedback: measure the real result often, compare with the goal, correct the process. Lesson 2
Empiricism
Knowledge comes from experience; decisions are based on what is observed (Scrum Guide 2020). Lesson 2
Lean thinking
Reducing waste and focusing on the essentials (Scrum Guide 2020). Lesson 2
Transparency
The process and the work are visible to those who do it and those who receive it. Lesson 2
Inspection
Frequent, diligent checks of the artifacts and of progress toward the goals, to detect undesirable variances. Lesson 2
Adaptation
Adjusting the process or the product as soon as a deviation outside acceptable limits is found. Lesson 2
Acceptable limit
A measurable tolerance agreed in advance; a result outside it is a deviation. Lesson 2
Scrum values
Commitment, focus, openness, respect, courage (in the Guide since 2016). Lesson 2
Product Owner
Accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team; one person, not a committee. Lesson 3
Value versus output
Output is the amount produced; value is its effect on users and on the organization. Lesson 3
Product Backlog management
Product Goal; creating and communicating items; ordering; keeping the backlog transparent, visible and understood. Lesson 3
Ordering
One sequence without ties, weighing value, size, risk and dependencies. Lesson 3
Stakeholder
A person affected by the product or able to affect it; influences the backlog by convincing the Product Owner. Lesson 3
Scrum Master
Accountable for establishing Scrum and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness; a true leader who serves. Lesson 3
Servant leadership
Leadership whose first priority is the growth and well-being of those served (Greenleaf, 1970). Lesson 3
Impediment
An obstacle to the team's progress that the Developers cannot remove by themselves in reasonable time. Lesson 3
Anti-pattern
A practice that looks reasonable but defeats the purpose of the rule it imitates. Lesson 3
Scrum Team
One Scrum Master, one Product Owner and Developers; no sub-teams or hierarchies; one objective at a time, the Product Goal. Lesson 4
Developers
The people in the Scrum Team committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint — not only programmers. Lesson 4
Cross-functional
The team has, together, all the skills needed to create value each Sprint. Lesson 4
Self-managing
The team internally decides who does what, when, and how (2020; before: self-organizing). Lesson 4
Communication path
A two-person channel inside a team; a team of n people has n × (n − 1) ÷ 2 of them. Lesson 4
Brooks's law
"Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later" (Brooks, 1975). Lesson 4
Team size
Typically 10 or fewer; larger efforts use several Scrum Teams sharing one Product Goal, one Product Backlog and one Product Owner. Lesson 4
Rotation (course rule)
Each member is Product Owner once and Scrum Master once before the Mid-Term — a learning device, not a Scrum rule. Lesson 4
