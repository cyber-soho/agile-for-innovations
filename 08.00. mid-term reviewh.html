    Lesson 1

    Chapter 1 review: Welcome to the Age of Agile

    0:05–0:13

    Review time 0:05 – 0:13 · Module 01.01, Class 1

    Chapter 1 answers two questions: how this course works, and what the word Agile means before we add any framework to it.

    1.1 Welcome and the rhythm

    Three words come back every week, so learn them exactly. A methodology is a documented, repeatable set of principles, roles, practices and artifacts that a group follows to plan, decide, deliver and learn. Agile names a family of methodologies that deliver work in small usable pieces, put those pieces in front of real users early and often, and change the plan on the basis of what the users do. Innovation, in the definition the 🔗 OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) · Oslo Manual 2018 · oecd.org adopted, is a new or improved product or process that differs significantly from what existed before and that has been made available to potential users. Read the last clause twice: an idea that never reaches a user is not yet an innovation.

    The course runs on the 1:2:2 rhythm: one hour of lecture (four beats, each a ten-minute lesson followed by a five-minute activity), two hours of Team Studio in class (planning 10 minutes, build about 80, Sprint Review 20, Retrospective 10), and two hours of Home Sprint (finish the deliverable, read, write the five-line Learning Log, submit on Blackboard by Sunday 23:59).

    Every block of that rhythm is a time-box: a fixed, maximum duration decided in advance. When it ends, the activity stops, finished or not, and the unfinished work is written down and carried into the next planning instead of being squeezed into overtime. Two facts justify it. C. Northcote Parkinson observed in 1955 that work expands to fill the time available. And a fixed cadence makes progress measurable: you can compare what was finished this week with what was finished last week. In Scrum every event is a time-box; the Sprint lasts one month or less and the Daily Scrum fifteen minutes.

    1.2 What is a methodology?

    Take the definition apart. Documented: a new member can read it. Repeatable: results become comparable. Principles: the reasons behind the rules. Roles: who decides what. Practices: the activities performed regularly. Artifacts: what the work produces and keeps. Plan, decide, deliver, learn: if one verb is missing, most often learn, you have a procedure, not a methodology.

    Four neighbouring words are easy to mix up. A method is a single technique (one way of estimating, for example). A process is an ordered sequence of activities with inputs and outputs. A framework is a deliberately minimal structure that leaves the techniques open; the 🔗 Scrum Guide · scrumguides.org calls Scrum a framework for this reason. A tool is software or equipment used inside a methodology; you can swap the tool and keep the methodology.

    Organizations need a methodology for four measurable reasons: consistency, coordination, learning and accountability. The mechanism method → culture → results has no poetry in it: a methodology decides which behaviours are rewarded; behaviours repeated by a group become its culture; the culture produces the results that employees, customers and stakeholders experience.

    Every methodology sits on one axis. The 🔗 PMI (Project Management Institute) · pmi.org names the two ends in its Agile Practice Guide. A predictive (plan-driven) approach fixes requirements, schedule and budget early, handles change through formal change control and delivers mostly at the end; the best-known predictive model is the Waterfall sequence (requirements, design, implementation, verification, maintenance), drawn by Winston Royce in 1970, who himself warned that a single pass was risky. An adaptive (change-driven) approach delivers a usable result in every short cycle and adjusts the plan from feedback; time and cost are fixed per cycle, and the scope flexes. A hybrid combines both. What decides is uncertainty about the problem and the solution. Predictive is the right choice for a bridge or the wiring of a building; digital products for real users are almost always high-uncertainty work. Predictive is not a synonym for wrong.

    Worked example from the module. Two teams build the same laundry-room page in fourteen weeks. The predictive team gets one real-user contact, a first usable result in week 14 and two weeks of rework after a change in week 6. The adaptive team gets seven user contacts, a first usable result in week 2 and half a week of rework.

    1.3 Agile in ten minutes

    The 🔗 Manifesto for Agile Software Development · agilemanifesto.org was written in February 2001 at Snowbird, Utah, by seventeen practitioners: four values, twelve principles. The mechanisms underneath are three terms.

    • An iteration is one short, fixed cycle of plan, build, review and adapt. In Scrum it is called a Sprint.
    • An increment is the usable result an iteration produces. Usable: a real person can do something with it (a slide deck about a feature is not an increment). Additive: it is added to everything delivered before and verified to work with it.
    • A feedback loop is deliver, observe real use, learn, adjust the plan. Barry Boehm's studies showed that the cost of correcting a wrong decision rises steeply the later it is discovered, so a short loop is the cheapest risk reduction a team can buy.

    The plan lives in the backlog: one ordered list, the most valuable item on top, changed after every review. Each iteration ends with two different inspections. The Sprint Review inspects the product with users and stakeholders; the Sprint Retrospective inspects the team and its way of working. "Agile means no planning" is false: Agile teams plan continuously, in smaller pieces, at the moments when they know the most.

    Denning's three laws were named here and developed in Chapter 2: the Law of the Small Team, the Law of the Customer and the Law of the Network. The Agile family includes Scrum, Kanban, Lean and DevOps (Development and Operations).

    1.4 The Integrative Mini-Project (the Portfolio)

    One product for users you can reach, built by one team, one deliverable a week (D1 to D11, Deliverables 1 to 11). D1 to D7 form your Mid-Term basis. The on-time rule: due Sunday 23:59; each piece counts three times (continuous assessment, Mid-Term basis, Final study base); minus 10 % per day late for up to three days, then zero but still required; keep your own copy. A formative assessment gives you information while there is time to act (fun-quizzes, flash-cards, self-checks); a summative one measures learning at a fixed point (Checkpoint Quizzes, Mid-Term, Final). A rubric is the published table of criteria and marks.

    Chapter 2 review: From Waterfall to Agile, the Manifesto and the mindset

    0:13–0:22

    Review time 0:13 – 0:22 · Module 02.01, Class 2

    Chapter 2 explains why plans made too early fail, what the Manifesto actually says, how its principles look in a team's behaviour, and why Agile works outside software.

    2.1 The problem with big plans

    Where the sequence comes from. Winston W. Royce's 1970 paper Managing the Development of Large Software Systems contains the staircase of seven boxes (system requirements, software requirements, analysis, program design, coding, testing, operations). The word "waterfall" is not in the paper; later authors attached it. Royce wrote that the simple sequence "is risky and invites failure", because testing comes last, and he recommended doing the sequence at least twice. The industry kept the drawing and ignored the advice. In 1985 the 🔗 United States Department of Defense · defense.gov published DOD-STD-2167 (Department of Defense Standard 2167), widely read as requiring phases with an approved document at the end of each; because defence contracts paid for much of the world's software, the sequence became the default.

    The cone of uncertainty. Measured by Barry Boehm (Software Engineering Economics, 1981) and popularized by Steve McConnell, whose company 🔗 Construx · construx.com keeps the reference description. At the idea (feasibility) stage the honest estimate is a range from one quarter to four times the eventual actual value (×/÷ 4). The range narrows to ×/÷ 2 once the concept of operation is agreed, ×/÷ 1.5 once requirements are accepted, ×/÷ 1.25 after product design, ×/÷ 1.1 after detailed design, and closes on the actual value when the work is finished. Two consequences matter. The cone narrows only when decisions and information remove variability, never with the calendar alone. And a sequential plan places the binding commitment point at the widest part of the cone, then forbids changing it, so every later discovery becomes a "failure of the plan" instead of information.

    The evidence. The 🔗 Standish Group · standishgroup.com has published the CHAOS report (a study name, not an abbreviation) since 1994: edition after edition, about one project in three finishes on time, on budget and with the planned scope, and small adaptive projects succeed more often.

    Snowbird, February 11 to 13, 2001. Practitioners of the "lightweight" methods, among them XP (Extreme Programming, Kent Beck), Scrum (Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber), DSDM (Dynamic Systems Development Method), FDD (Feature-Driven Development), Crystal and Adaptive Software Development, met at a Utah ski resort after invitations from Robert C. Martin. Seventeen of them agreed on the word Agile (replacing "lightweight") and on the four values. The twelve principles were finished by e-mail in the following months, and the group founded the non-profit 🔗 Agile Alliance · agilealliance.org later in 2001. The Manifesto is not a method: it prescribes no meeting, role or document.

    2.2 The four values: "over", not "instead of"

    The four values, as published at 🔗 agilemanifesto.org (© 2001, the seventeen authors):

    We value more (left) over Still valued (right)
    Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
    Working software over comprehensive documentation
    Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
    Responding to change over following a plan

    The closing sentence gives the reading rule: there is value in the items on the right, but the items on the left are valued more. "Over" expresses a priority, not an exclusion. When the two sides conflict, the left wins; the right side is still done. A team that writes no documentation, signs nothing and plans nothing has misread the text.

    Why each left side wins. Understanding between people is produced by interaction, and a process cannot notice that two people mean different things by one word. Working software is evidence, while a document is a prediction; the Manifesto objects to comprehensive (documentation for its own completeness), not to documentation. A contract signed at the widest point of the cone fixes both parties' ignorance, while collaboration lets them use what they learn; Agile contracts fix budget and cadence and leave detailed scope open. A plan is a hypothesis; Agile teams plan in short horizons and re-plan at fixed intervals.

    All four right sides can be inspected and controlled from outside, in advance (a tool, a document, a contract, a plan); all four left sides exist only between people while the work is done. The D2 (Deliverable 2) Manifesto map turns each value into one observable practice with a frequency ("we demonstrate the increment to two real users at the end of every iteration") and keeps something on each right pan.

    2.3 Twelve principles, four themes, and the cycle that carries them

    The course groups the twelve principles (full text at 🔗 agilemanifesto.org/principles) into four themes, each principle read as observable behaviour:

    Theme Principles What you can observe
    Customer and value 1, 2, 4 Something usable reaches users early and often (1); late change is welcomed and re-ordered (2); business people and developers talk daily (4)
    People and interaction 5, 6, 11 Motivated people are trusted to decide how (5); questions are asked face to face first (6); designs emerge from self-organizing teams (11)
    Working product and feedback 3, 7, 12 Delivery every couple of weeks (3); progress reported as what users can now do (7); reflection and one real change at regular intervals (12)
    Sustainability and excellence 8, 9, 10 Constant pace, no crunch (8); tests, reviews, shortcuts repaid (9); simplicity, the art of maximizing work not done (10)

    Iteration describes the cycle; increment describes the output. A team that repeats a cycle without adding anything usable is iterating without incrementing (refining); a team that adds pieces without a fixed cycle is incrementing without iterating (accumulating without a review point). Agile methods do both. A feedback loop comes from control engineering: measure the output, compare it with the goal, correct the next input. "Fail fast, learn faster" means precisely this: find wrong assumptions while little is built on them; the value lies in the low cost of the discovery, not in the failure. In Scrum the container is the Sprint: principles 1, 3 and 7 are the increment; 2 and 4 are the Product Owner in the Review; 5, 6 and 11 are the Developers deciding how; 12 is the Retrospective; 8, 9 and 10 are the Definition of Done and the amount of work taken on.

    2.4 Denning's three laws: Small Team, Customer, Network

    Stephen Denning (🔗 stevedenning.com), author of The Age of Agile (🔗 HarperCollins Leadership · harpercollinsleadership.com, 2018), a former director of knowledge management at the World Bank, studied organizations where Agile worked at scale and found three regularities that he calls laws (first stated in his 2016 article, 🔗 Strategy & Leadership · doi.org).

    • Law of the Small Team (Chapter 2 of the book): work done by small, autonomous, cross-functional teams, in short cycles, on relatively small tasks, with continuous feedback from the end user. Remove one condition and the effect of the others disappears. Example: 🔗 Menlo Innovations · menloinnovations.com in Ann Arbor, with pairs that change weekly and one-week cycles. Amazon's rule of thumb: a team two pizzas can feed.
    • Law of the Customer (Chapter 3): delivering value to customers is the purpose of the whole organization. Denning calls it a Copernican revolution in management: the customer, not the firm, moves to the centre. He traces it to Peter Drucker (1954) and explains why it became unavoidable: globalization, deregulation and the Internet moved market power from seller to buyer.
    • Law of the Network (Chapter 4): the organization works as a transparent network of teams, not a top-down bureaucracy. It exists because excellent small teams left inside an unchanged hierarchy are absorbed or dissolved within two or three years. Hierarchy does not disappear; requests travel directly between the teams that need them. Examples: 🔗 Spotify engineering culture · engineering.atspotify.com (squads, tribes, chapters, guilds) and 🔗 Barclays · home.barclays. Its measurable sign is short: few hand-offs and few days between a customer's request and its resolution, the lead time of Chapter 6.

    The three laws are needed together: small teams without customer focus optimize themselves; customer focus without small teams cannot deliver; both without a network are crushed by the surrounding bureaucracy. The environment that makes them necessary is VUCA (Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, Ambiguity), a term from the 🔗 U.S. Army War College · armywarcollege.edu: in a stable world a bureaucracy is efficient; in a VUCA world it is late.

    Agile beyond software. The 🔗 Agile Marketing Manifesto · agilemarketingmanifesto.org (San Francisco, June 2012); 🔗 eduScrum · eduscrum.org (Willy Wijnands, the Netherlands, 2011); and the hotel example of the module, where a guest's Wi-Fi complaint climbs a pyramid for three days or is solved in an hour by a small cross-functional team with a two-hundred-dollar authority.

    3.1 Where Scrum comes from, and the whole framework on one page

    The 🔗 Scrum Guide 2020 · scrumguides.org (Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, offered under 🔗 CC BY-SA 4.0 (Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0) · creativecommons.org) defines Scrum in one sentence: a lightweight framework that helps people, teams and organizations generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems. Lightweight: the whole Guide is about thirteen pages. Framework: a minimal set of mandatory elements, purposefully incomplete, that the team completes with its own practices. Complex problem: requirements and means are not fully known in advance, so results are known only after trying.

    Seven dates.

    Year Event
    1986 Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka publish "The New New Product Development Game" in the 🔗 HBR (Harvard Business Review) · hbr.org: one multidisciplinary team with overlapping phases instead of sequential hand-offs; they name the approach after the rugby scrum
    1993 Jeff Sutherland's team at Easel Corporation (a company that no longer exists) runs the first Scrum team in software (🔗 InfoQ, 2006 · infoq.com)
    1995 Schwaber and Sutherland present Scrum at OOPSLA (Object-Oriented Programming, Systems, Languages & Applications) '95 in Austin, Texas, a conference of the 🔗 ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) · acm.org; Schwaber's paper calls systems development "an unpredictable, complicated process"
    2001 Both authors co-sign the Agile Manifesto
    2010 First Scrum Guide; revisions in 2011, 2013, 2016, 2017 and 2020 (🔗 revision history · scrumguides.org)
    2016 The five Scrum values are added
    2020 November 18: the current edition; one Scrum Team (no team within a team), "Developers", self-managing, the new Product Goal, one commitment per artifact

    The framework as 3 · 5 · 3. Three accountabilities: the Product Owner (the value of the product), the Scrum Master (Scrum understood and used, and the team's effectiveness), the Developers (a usable Increment every Sprint). Five events, each a time-box: the Sprint (one month or less, containing the other four), Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum (15 minutes), the Sprint Review, the Sprint Retrospective. Three artifacts, each with one commitment: Product Backlog → Product Goal; Sprint Backlog → Sprint Goal; Increment → Definition of Done. The 2020 Guide says accountability rather than role: a role is a title on a badge; an accountability is a result that one person must answer for.

    3.2 Empiricism: the three pillars and the five values

    Defined process control works when every step is known and the same inputs give the same outputs (a bottling line, compiling the same code twice): plan once, check at the end. Empirical process control works when unknown factors make outputs vary: measure the real result often, compare it with the goal, correct the process. Scrum chooses the second. The Guide calls it empiricism (knowledge comes from experience and decisions are based on what is observed) and adds lean thinking (reduce waste, focus on the essentials). The length of the loop matters: with two-week Sprints at most ten working days of work rest on a wrong assumption before the Review exposes it; with a six-month plan, about 120.

    The three pillars, in this order. Transparency: the work and the process are visible to those who do it and those who receive it. Inspection: artifacts and progress toward the goals are checked frequently and diligently to detect undesirable variances; the five events are the formal inspection points. Adaptation: when something deviates outside acceptable limits, the process or the product is adjusted as soon as possible. Transparency makes inspection possible (inspection without it is "misleading and wasteful"); inspection makes adaptation possible (inspection without adaptation is "pointless"). An acceptable limit is a measurable tolerance agreed in advance; a result outside it is a deviation.

    The five values (in the Guide since 2016): commitment, focus, openness, respect, courage. Each keeps one part of the loop working: openness produces transparency; courage makes honest inspection possible; commitment and focus turn an inspection into a real change; respect allows a team to manage itself.

    Module numbers worth remembering: with weekly inspection and adaptation, Team A let 4 wrong allergen entries reach students in six weeks; Team B, checking once in week 6, let 18 through. Same people, same skills; only the loop differed.

    3.3 The Product Owner and the Scrum Master

    The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team. Value is the effect on users and the organization; output is the amount produced. Product Backlog management has four parts: developing and explicitly communicating the Product Goal; creating and communicating items; ordering them; keeping the backlog transparent, visible and understood. Ordering means one sequence without ties, weighing value, size, risk and dependencies; a common heuristic is value per point (value ÷ size), with dependencies applied before the formula. The Product Owner is one person, not a committee; stakeholders change the backlog by convincing the Product Owner. Anti-patterns: the absent Product Owner, the committee, the proxy without authority, the Product Owner who tells Developers how to work.

    The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the Guide and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness; the 2020 Guide calls Scrum Masters "true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the larger organization" (until 2017 it said servant-leader, from Robert K. Greenleaf's 1970 essay; 🔗 Greenleaf Center for Servant Leadership · greenleaf.org). The Scrum Master serves the team (coaching self-management, causing the removal of impediments, keeping events positive and inside their time-box), the Product Owner (techniques for the Product Goal and backlog) and the organization (leading Scrum adoption). An impediment is an obstacle the Developers cannot remove by themselves in reasonable time; causing its removal means making sure it disappears, not necessarily doing the repair personally. The Scrum Master is not a project manager. Anti-patterns: the boss, the secretary, the hero, the absentee.

    3.4 The Developers and the Scrum Team

    One Scrum Team: one Scrum Master, one Product Owner and Developers; no sub-teams or hierarchies; one objective at a time, the Product Goal. The Developers are the people committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint: designers, testers, writers and programmers alike. Their four accountabilities: creating the Sprint Backlog plan; instilling quality by adhering to the Definition of Done; adapting their plan each day toward the Sprint Goal; holding each other accountable as professionals. In 2020 "Development Team" became "Developers" to remove the team within a team.

    Cross-functional: together the members have all the skills to create value each Sprint (tested at team level, not per person). Self-managing: the team internally decides who does what, when and how (before 2020, self-organizing, which covered mainly the how).

    Size: typically 10 or fewer. Every pair of people is one communication path: paths = n × (n − 1) ÷ 2. Values: 2 → 1, 4 → 6, 5 → 10, 7 → 21, 10 → 45, 15 → 105. People grow linearly; paths grow with the square. Fred Brooks (The Mythical Man-Month, 1975; 🔗 ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) Turing Award 1999 · amturing.acm.org) stated Brooks's law: adding manpower to a late software project makes it later. When a product needs more people, Scrum creates several Scrum Teams that share one Product Goal, one Product Backlog and one Product Owner. A Product Owner or Scrum Master who works on Sprint Backlog items participates as a Developer. The course's rotation (everyone Product Owner once and Scrum Master once before the Mid-Term) is a learning device, not a Scrum rule.

    4.1 The Sprint and Sprint Planning

    A Sprint is a fixed-length event of one month or less, and the next one starts immediately after the previous one ends: fixed length, maximum one month, consecutive. In this course Sprints last two weeks (Sprint 1: October 12 to 25). A shorter Sprint lowers risk because fewer days of work can rest on a wrong assumption; when the horizon is too long, the Guide warns, the Sprint Goal may become invalid, complexity may rise and risk may increase. There is no "Sprint 0" and no testing Sprint in the Guide: every Sprint produces a usable Increment.

    Four rules while the Sprint runs: no changes are made that would endanger the Sprint Goal; quality does not decrease; the Product Backlog is refined as needed; scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner as more is learned. The scope (the list of items) may move; the Sprint Goal (the single objective) is protected. A Sprint may be cancelled only by the Product Owner, and only when the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete; a difficult Sprint is not a reason.

    Forecasting charts (burn-downs, burn-ups, cumulative flows) help but, in the Guide's words, do not replace the importance of empiricism: a chart forecasts; only an inspection of the real Increment tells you what is Done.

    Sprint Planning opens the Sprint; the whole Scrum Team attends; the time-box is at most eight hours for a one-month Sprint, usually shorter for shorter Sprints. Three topics: Why is this Sprint valuable? (the Product Owner proposes; the whole team defines the Sprint Goal). What can be Done? (the Developers select items after discussion with the Product Owner; they are responsible for sizing; an item is ready when it can be Done within one Sprint). How will the work get done? (the Developers alone plan it, often as work items of one day or less; two hours or less in this course). The three answers together form the Sprint Backlog. Capacity can be counted in hours (people × hours × days, minus the events) or in points against past velocity.

    Module numbers: Team Menu took 10 points as a cautious capacity and selected four items for 9 points; available time was 4 members × 4 hours per week × 2 weeks − 3 hours of events = 29 hours; fourteen work items needed 20 hours, leaving a 9-hour margin. On day 8, the French version proved three times bigger; scope was renegotiated with the Product Owner and the Sprint Goal held in English. Skipping the second-person check was refused: quality does not decrease.

    4.2 The Daily Scrum and the Sprint Review

    The Daily Scrum inspects progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapts the Sprint Backlog; it is a 15-minute event for the Developers, at the same time and place every working day; its output is an actionable plan for the next day. The format is free, as long as it focuses on the Sprint Goal (the three questions of the 2013 to 2017 Guides are one option). Anti-patterns: the status report to a manager and the problem-solving session while others wait (people can meet again right after). The course accepts an asynchronous Daily Scrum three times a week as an adaptation.

    The Sprint Review inspects the outcome of the Sprint and determines future adaptations; the Scrum Team presents results to key stakeholders and progress toward the Product Goal is discussed; it is the second-to-last event; the time-box is at most four hours for a one-month Sprint. It is a working session, not a presentation: the Product Backlog may be adjusted there. An item that does not meet the Definition of Done cannot be released or even presented at the Review. The Review is never a gate to releasing value: an Increment can be delivered as soon as it is Done.

    4.3 The Sprint Retrospective, and the three artifacts with their commitments

    The Sprint Retrospective plans ways to increase quality and effectiveness. The Scrum Team inspects individuals, interactions, processes, tools and its Definition of Done; it is the last event and concludes the Sprint; at most three hours for a one-month Sprint; stakeholders are not invited. The most impactful improvements are addressed as soon as possible and may be added to the next Sprint Backlog. Formats: Start / Stop / Continue, 4Ls (liked, learned, lacked, longed for), Mad / Sad / Glad. Three rules make it useful: use data, choose one or two improvements, give each an owner and a measure. The Scrum Master facilitates and is inspected like everyone else.

    Artifacts represent work or value and maximize transparency; each carries a commitment against which progress is measured.

    Artifact What it is Commitment
    Product Backlog An emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product; the single source of work Product Goal: a future state of the product to plan against; one at a time
    Sprint Backlog Sprint Goal (why) + selected items (what) + actionable plan (how); by and for the Developers Sprint Goal: the single objective of the Sprint

    Refinement breaks items into smaller, more precise ones; the Developers size them. Several Increments may be created in one Sprint: the moment an item meets the Definition of Done, an Increment is born. The Review inspects them; it does not create them.

    4.4 The Definition of Done

    The Definition of Done is a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product. It creates transparency: everyone shares one understanding of what was completed. An item that fails it returns to the Product Backlog. An organizational standard, if one exists, is the minimum; several Scrum Teams on one product share one Definition of Done.

    Done versus acceptance criteria. The Definition of Done is the same checklist for every item (is it finished to our standard?); acceptance criteria belong to one item (does it do what it is meant to do?). An item needs both. A skipped check leaves undone work, and accumulated undone work is one source of technical debt.

    A good Definition of Done has five to eight checks that are observable (a second person can verify), realistic (achievable in one Sprint) and shared (one list, visible on the board). A sample for a student software product covers the code (in the shared repository, reviewed), behaviour (criteria met, tests pass), users (phone and laptop, user note), deployment (on a test link), acceptance (Product Owner accepted) and data and rules (no outside personal data, licensed content). The standard may be raised at a Retrospective; it is never lowered to finish an item.

    Module numbers: of Team Menu's four Sprint 1 items, three were Done; the first Increment was born on Friday, October 16 and the second on Tuesday, October 20, both before the Review of October 25; the French version failed and returned to the Product Backlog.

    A note before the content: the Scrum Guide requires an ordered Product Backlog that is refined (description, order, size). It does not prescribe user stories, story points, velocity or burndown charts. These are common practices around Scrum, each with a named author, and this chapter uses them because they teach estimation and inspection (see 🔗 Scrum.org, "Myth 9: Story Points are Required in Scrum" · scrum.org).

    5.1 Stories, not specifications

    The usual form, made standard by Mike Cohn (User Stories Applied, 2004; 🔗 Mountain Goat Software · mountaingoatsoftware.com), from an XP (Extreme Programming) practice of the late 1990s (🔗 Agile Alliance glossary · agilealliance.org):

    As a [role], I want [goal], so that [benefit].

    Three parts, three rules. The role is one real user (never "the system", "the team" or "everyone"). The goal is an action or ability, stated without the technique that will deliver it ("a dropdown with six colours" has already decided the design). The benefit must not repeat the goal; it tells the team which problem it solves, and that is what lets the team find a cheaper way to the same result. A story is deliberately short: a placeholder for a conversation, not a specification.

    The three Cs (Ron Jeffries, 2001; 🔗 ronjeffries.com): the Card (the written sentence, a reminder of a promise to talk), the Conversation (where the Product Owner and the Developers discover the details; most understanding is created here), the Confirmation (the acceptance test).

    Three sizes (common practice): an epic is too big for one Sprint and hides several benefits; it is split before it is estimated. A story is one benefit for one user, finished in well under one Sprint. A task is a work item of the Sprint Backlog plan that builds part of a story and delivers nothing on its own. Only stories are estimated and counted in velocity.

    5.2 INVEST and splitting

    Bill Wake's 2003 checklist INVEST (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable; 🔗 xp123.com) reads each letter as a question, and each "no" names its fix:

    Letter Question Fix when the answer is "no"
    Independent Can it be delivered without waiting for another story? Re-order, or merge stories that cannot be separated
    Negotiable Does it leave the how open? Delete the technical details; keep the benefit
    Valuable Does a user gain something from it alone? Rewrite the "so that"; if impossible, it is a task
    Estimable Can the Developers size it roughly? Run a spike first
    Small Can it be finished in well under one Sprint? Split it
    Testable Can another person decide it is done? Write the acceptance test

    Independent is about delivery, not about shared code. Valuable is judged from the user's side. The lab rule allows at most one ✗ per story, with a written reason; two ✗ mean rewrite. A spike is a short, time-boxed research task whose only output is knowledge; it makes a story estimable, delivers nothing to a user and is not counted in velocity.

    Splitting. Every slice must still be a story, a vertical slice thin but complete from the user's action to the result; a technical layer ("the database part") delivers nothing alone. Five patterns: by workflow step; by business rule; by data variation; happy path versus edge cases; by user or role. Mike Cohn's SPIDR (Spikes, Paths, Interfaces, Data, Rules; 🔗 story splitting · mountaingoatsoftware.com) names the same ideas. Classic diagnoses: "the whole admin module" fails S first; "the app should be nice" fails T first; "as a developer I want to refactor the database" fails V first.

    5.3 Acceptance criteria and story points

    An acceptance test (acceptance criteria) is the agreed way to show that one story works, written in this course as one line with Dan North's words from BDD (Behaviour-Driven Development, 2006; 🔗 dannorth.net), also the keywords of Gherkin (🔗 Cucumber · cucumber.io): Given [the starting situation], when [one action], then [one observable result]. Observable means a person or a tool can see, count or measure it without asking the author ("three meal names are shown"); "works well" and "fast enough" are not observable.

    Story points measure relative size: effort, complexity and uncertainty together, against a 1-point reference story. Not hours, for three reasons: hours depend on who does the work; people compare better than they measure; hours turn into promises. A velocity in points belongs to one team and is never compared between teams. The scale is Fibonacci-like (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, sometimes 20, 40, 100) because uncertainty grows with size; this course stops at 8 (bigger fails S). T-shirt sizes (XS, extra small, to XL, extra large) are the same idea on a coarser scale.

    Planning Poker (James Grenning, 2002; 🔗 the paper · wingman-sw.com; rules at 🔗 mountaingoatsoftware.com) defeats anchoring, the pull of the first number spoken. The Product Owner reads the story and its test; questions for about thirty seconds; everyone reveals at the same moment. Votes within one step of the scale: take the majority. More than one step apart: the lowest and highest voters explain, the team votes again (at most twice), and if it still disagrees takes the higher number. A flat hand turned sideways means "cannot estimate yet" and sends the story to a spike.

    5.4 Velocity, capacity, burndown and the release forecast

    Velocity is the sum of the story points of the stories that met the Definition of Done in a Sprint; a partly finished story counts zero. For forecasting, average the last three Sprints. Example: 18, 22, 20 → velocity 20; 120 points left → 6 Sprints; 130 points → 6.5 → 7 (always round up: a partial Sprint costs a whole Sprint). Velocity is one team's number, never a target, and a forecast, never a promise. A first Sprint uses the planned velocity until the real one is known.

    Capacity is time: people × focused hours per day × working days. Example: 4 × 6 × 9 = 216 hours. Capacity and velocity are different numbers and are never converted into each other.

    Burndown chart: points remaining against days. The ideal line runs from the Sprint total on day 0 to zero on the last day: remaining at day d = total × (days − d) ÷ days (40 points, 10 days → 4 points per day). The actual line is the total minus the points of stories that met the Definition of Done. Above the ideal line = behind; below = ahead; flat then a sudden drop = stories finished one at a time at the end because too many were started; a rise = scope added, which needs a written note.

    MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time), from the DSDM (Dynamic Systems Development Method) community and its owner the 🔗 Agile Business Consortium · agilebusiness.org: Must = if missing, do we cancel the release? Should = painful to leave out but users can wait a Sprint. Could = nobody would complain loudly. Won't (this time) = deliberately not now, with a written reason. Keep the Musts to about 60 % of the points at most.

    The release forecast in five lines. Release scope = Must + Should points. Remaining = scope − Must and Should points already Done (or planned in Sprint 1). Planned forecast = remaining ÷ planned velocity, rounded up. Cautious forecast = remaining ÷ (0.7 × planned velocity, rounded down), rounded up. Dates = the Sprint ends (October 25, November 8, November 22, December 6, December 20 in this course).

    Team Menu's numbers: Must 7 points (29 % of 24), Should 9, Could 3, Won't 5; scope 16; Sprint 1 planned 9; planned forecast 7 ÷ 9 = 0.78 → 1 Sprint (November 8); cautious 9 × 0.7 = 6.3 → 6, 7 ÷ 6 = 1.17 → 2 Sprints (November 22); real velocity 6 (story 4 counted zero); updated forecast (16 − 6) ÷ 6 = 1.67 → 2 Sprints (November 22).

    Three words from Denning's Chapter 5 (Microsoft's move to Agile, 🔗 microsoft.com): technical debt, a shortcut now that slows later work (Ward Cunningham, 1992; 🔗 c2.com); CI (Continuous Integration), work combined into a shared repository at least daily with an automated build and tests (🔗 Martin Fowler · martinfowler.com); DevOps (Development and Operations), builders and operators working together so software reaches users in small, frequent steps.

    Two free guides use the word Kanban: the 🔗 Kanban Guide · kanbanguides.org (John Coleman and Daniel Vacanti, edition 2025.5), which defines the flow measures, and the 🔗 Kanban University guide to the Kanban Method · kanban.university (David J. Anderson, 2010), which gives the board vocabulary.

    6.1 The board and the pull

    Kanban is Japanese for signboard. In the TPS (Toyota Production System; 🔗 Toyota Motor Corporation · global.toyota), a kanban is a card on a container of parts: when the parts are used, the card travels back to the preceding process as the order to make one more (🔗 Toyota UK (United Kingdom) Magazine · mag.toyota.co.uk). The TPS rests on two pillars: jidoka (stop when something abnormal is detected) and JIT (Just-in-Time: only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed). Kiichiro Toyoda formulated JIT; Taiichi Ohno built the framework between the 1950s and 1970s and wrote that the idea came from watching an American supermarket shelf being restocked. Three properties survived the trip to software: work is pulled by consumption; the signal is visual and sits on the work; the number of cards is fixed, which caps inventory (the first WIP (Work In Progress) limit in history).

    Into knowledge work. Anderson describes the first pull system for knowledge work at a Microsoft group, XIT, in 2004 (with Dragos Dumitriu), then a card wall at Corbis in 2006 and 2007, the Kanban Method from 2007 and the book in 2010 (🔗 djaa.com). A software card is a different piece of work every time; what is kept is the pull, the visual signal and the cap. Kanban University lists six practices: visualize; limit WIP; manage flow; make policies explicit; implement feedback loops; improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally. The Kanban Guide calls Kanban a strategy for optimizing the flow of value through a process, with three practices (define and visualize a workflow, actively manage items, improve the workflow). Neither guide prescribes roles, events or artifacts; Kanban is added to what you already do ("start with what you do now"), Scrum included.

    The board is the visible form of the DoW (Definition of Workflow), whose six elements are: a definition of the work items; when an item is started and finished; the states between them (the columns); how WIP is controlled; explicit policies for each state; an SLE (Service Level Expectation). The commitment point is where the team decides it will do the item (entering Ready); the lead-time clock starts there. The delivery point is where the item is usable (entering Done after all Definition of Done checks); the clocks stop there. A policy says who, what and how many ("Review: a member who did not build it compares every entry with the card; at most one card"). Every card carries its dates.

    The pull rule: work on an item starts only when there is a clear signal of capacity, which on a board is an empty slot under the column's limit. On a push board work is handed on regardless of capacity and piles up invisibly in front of the slowest person.

    6.2 WIP and WIP limits

    WIP (Work In Progress) is the number of work items started but not finished: a count of items, not of hours, points or people. Each open item costs attention, ages (its information goes stale) and delays feedback.

    Why starting everything makes everything late. Three equal stories of three days each, all committed on day 0. Start all three and split the effort: each gets one third of a day per day, so all finish on day 9; lead times 9, 9, 9, average 9, first delivery day 9. One at a time: finish days 3, 6, 9; average 6; first delivery day 3. Same total work, same last day; a third less average waiting and a first delivery three times earlier, before counting any switching cost. Switching does cost: the 🔗 APA (American Psychological Association) · apa.org summarizes Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans (2001): goal shifting and rule activation cost time, and repeated switching was estimated at up to 40 % of productive time in some scenarios. Hence the Kanban sentence from Arne Roock's 2012 booklet: stop starting, start finishing.

    A WIP limit is a number written on a column (or on the whole board between the two points), an explicit policy changed only by decision. The Kanban Guide asks the system to operate neither above nor below the agreed control, and a limit is what creates the pull. A first limit comes from observation; for a small team, below the number of people (🔗 Atlassian, WIP limits · atlassian.com). A full column is a signal to finish: pair on the oldest card, take the card waiting in Review, remove a blocker, or improve the policy (swarming). It is never a reason to stand idle and never a punishment.

    Module numbers (Team Menu, 9 points, one team-day per point): WIP 4 → finish days 4, 7, 9, 9, average lead 7.25, first finish day 4; WIP 2 → 2, 4, 8, 9, average 5.75, first day 2; WIP 1 → 1, 3, 6, 9, average 4.75, first day 1. With a 20 % switching cost per extra item, WIP 4 slips to an average of 15.25 days and WIP 2 to 7.75; WIP 1 does not change.

    6.3 Lead time, cycle time, throughput, Little's Law

    Three clocks on one card. Cycle time = elapsed time from started (entered In progress) to finished (entered Done). Work item age = elapsed time from started to today, for an item still open. Lead time = elapsed time from the commitment point (entered Ready) to the delivery point (Done), the clock the person who was promised the item experiences. All are elapsed calendar days, including days nobody touched the card. The course convention: a duration = later date − earlier date (Monday → Friday = 4 days); some teams count both ends; write yours in the DoW (Definition of Workflow). Always ask: from when, and counted how?

    Throughput = the number of work items finished per unit of time, an exact count with the period stated. It is not velocity: throughput counts items, velocity sums points.

    Little's Law. John D. C. Little proved in 1961 that in a stable queue L = λW (🔗 Operations Research · doi.org). In flow form: average WIP (Work In Progress) = throughput × average cycle time, so average cycle time = average WIP ÷ throughput. Example: 8 items ÷ 2 items per day = 4 days. Units must match: 15 items ÷ 5 items per week = 3 weeks. The law is exact only in a stable system; Vacanti lists five conditions: arrival rate equals departure rate; every item that enters eventually leaves; WIP about the same at the start and end of the period; the average age of WIP neither growing nor shrinking; consistent units. The practical consequence: at the same throughput, less WIP means shorter cycle times.

    Team Menu, Sprint 1: lead and cycle times 4/3, 7/7, 10/10 days for the three finished stories, story 4 open at age 10; throughput 3 ÷ 14 ≈ 0.21 per day (1.5 per week); daily WIP summed to 31 card-days, average 2.2; Little predicted 2.2 ÷ 0.21 ≈ 10.3 days against 6.7 observed. The gap is the result to report: the window started at 0, ended with one open card, and held only four cards.

    6.4 The cumulative flow diagram, and Scrum vs. Kanban vs. Scrumban

    A CFD (Cumulative Flow Diagram) plots, day by day, the cumulative number of items that have entered each state, as stacked bands (finished at the bottom). Lines only rise or stay flat. Vertical thickness of a band = items in that state that day (the middle band is the WIP). Horizontal distance between the committed line and the finished line = approximate lead time. Slope of the bottom line = throughput. A widening band is a bottleneck: items enter that state faster than they leave. A flat bottom line means nothing is being finished.

    The SLE (Service Level Expectation) is a forecast of how long an item should take from started to finished, with two parts, a period and a probability, based on historical cycle time: "85 % of our stories finish within 8 days of being started". A forecast, not a promise; with three data points it stays provisional.

    7.1 Method → organization → product

    The way an organization arranges its work fixes how often customers receive something new and how soon it learns whether it helps. A team releasing a usable increment every two weeks can learn up to 26 times a year (52 ÷ 2); a department releasing once a year, once. Denning's cases (🔗 Drucker Forum blog, 2016 · druckerforum.org): before 2011, 🔗 Ericsson · ericsson.com worked on a five-year cycle; after its move to Agile, less work in progress and deployments one to two years earlier. At 🔗 Spotify · spotify.com, Discover Weekly came from one of more than a hundred small teams; 🔗 Barclays · home.barclays made Agile a strategic initiative in 2015.

    Conway's Law (Melvin Conway, April 1968; 🔗 melconway.com): organizations that design systems produce designs that copy their communication structures. Three departments that rarely talk produce three parts that connect badly. 🔗 Amazon · aboutamazon.com's two-pizza team rule (🔗 AWS (Amazon Web Services) Executive Insights · aws.amazon.com): no team larger than two pizzas can feed (under about ten people), each owning one product or service end to end; when work grows, split the team. The reason is paths: 4 people → 6, 12 people → 66.

    Scrum's path to the digital workplace. 1986, 1995, 2001, 2010, then the 2020 Guide, made "even less prescriptive" and stripped of remaining IT (Information Technology) wording (🔗 revisions · scrumguides.org). Around Scrum: CI (Continuous Integration: integrate into a shared repository at least daily, checked by an automated build and tests); CD (Continuous Delivery: get changes of all types to users quickly, safely and sustainably, so a release is a business decision; 🔗 continuousdelivery.com); DevOps (Development and Operations: builders and operators as one team). The Guide requires none of them and prescribes no location. 🔗 DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment) · dora.dev measures change lead time (from committed to version control to deployed in production) and deployment frequency.

    Product and service. The product is what users receive; the service is the continuing activity that keeps value reaching them (someone enters the menu before 11:00 every day). The four-box value map: who builds, who uses, who pays or carries the cost, and what flows along each arrow; an empty box is a finding ("unknown: ask whom, by when"). Team Menu's last arrow had a legal reason: allergen information for non-prepacked food in the EU (European Union) under 🔗 Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Article 44 · eur-lex.europa.eu.

    The innovation triangle. 🔗 IDEO · ideo.com describes design thinking as the search for solutions that are desirable, feasible and viable (🔗 IDEO Design Thinking · designthinking.ideo.com). The course puts one framework on each corner: desirability (do people want it? Design Thinking, Classes 10 to 13), viability (can it sustain itself? Lean Startup and the Business Model Canvas), feasibility (can we build and deliver it in increments? Scrum and Kanban). The triangle says which framework to reach for first.

    7.2 Team execution and the 3E's (Empower, Experiment, Evolve)

    Team execution means turning a goal into a usable result, again and again, under uncertainty. The 3E's are this course's working framing, not a published framework. An E sentence passes two tests: it names an action the team takes, and it carries a date, an event or an owner.

    • Empower: a goal and the authority to reach it. The Scrum Guide's cross-functional and self-managing teams; Marty Cagan's distinction (🔗 SVPG (Silicon Valley Product Group) · svpg.com) between an empowered team (receives a problem and owns the result) and a feature team (receives features already designed); Amazon's single-threaded leaders; the stream-aligned team of 🔗 Team Topologies · teamtopologies.com.
    • Experiment: small, cheap, reversible tests. Jeff Bezos's 🔗 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders (PDF) separates Type 1 decisions (consequential, irreversible or nearly so; decide slowly) from Type 2 decisions (changeable, reversible; decide quickly in small groups). An experiment has five parts: a question, a prediction written before, a countable measure, a time limit, a decision rule fixed in advance.
    • Evolve: inspection and adaptation on a rhythm (Scrum's pillars; Kanban University's sixth practice, "improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally"): the same numbers read at every Review, exactly one written policy changed per Retrospective.

    Discovery and delivery (Cagan, 🔗 Dual-Track Agile, 2012 · svpg.com): discovery quickly produces validated backlog items; delivery produces releasable product. They run in parallel, by the same team; Cagan now speaks of continuous discovery and delivery. Four product questions: is it valuable, usable, feasible, viable? Both kinds of card sit on one board; the course suggests a WIP (Work In Progress) limit of one for discovery cards.

    7.3 Lean Startup and the Business Model Canvas

    Eric Ries's Lean Startup (2011; 🔗 theleanstartup.com) makes experiments the main activity of any team creating something new under uncertainty. The loop Build–Measure–Learn: build the smallest testable product; measure what real users do; learn whether to persevere or pivot; go round as fast as possible. The unit of progress is validated learning. Inside Scrum, the Sprint gives the rhythm, the MVP test is a discovery card and the Sprint Review is the Learn step.

    The riskiest assumption is the belief that, if false, makes the product pointless (usually about user behaviour). The MVP (Minimum Viable Product) is the version that collects the maximum validated learning with the least effort; it is defined by its question, not its polish. The course's hypothesis template extends the one in Lean UX (User Experience) by Jeff Gothelf and Josh Seiden (🔗 jeffgothelf.com): We believe that [segment] will [behaviour] because [reason]. We will know we are right when we see [metric] reach [number] by [date]. Six parts; without number and date it cannot fail. Actionable metrics support a decision; vanity metrics grow whatever you do ("total visits since September"). The decision rule is written before the test. Then persevere, or pivot: a structured change of strategy that tests a new fundamental hypothesis while the vision stays (types include customer segment, channel, customer need and zoom-in).

    The Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder and Pigneur, 2010; 🔗 Strategyzer · strategyzer.com) shows on one page how a business creates, delivers and captures value, in nine blocks:

    Right side: customers and value Left side: infrastructure Bottom: money
    Customer Segments · Value Propositions (centre) · Channels · Customer Relationships Key Activities · Key Resources · Key Partnerships Cost Structure · Revenue Streams

    The Value Proposition Canvas zooms into one segment: jobs, pains and gains against products, pain relievers and gain creators. The course's canvas v0 fills the five customer-side blocks (Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams); v1 adds the other four in Studio 2 (December 7). Every value proposition states a gain, not a feature.

    Team Menu's test: a QR (Quick Response) sign linking to a hand-updated page, four lunch services; rule: persevere if the link is opened at least 30 times on at least three of four days. Scenario A (41, 28, 36, 33) → 3 of 4 → persevere. Scenario B (12, 9, 15, 11) → 0 of 4 → pivot candidate (channel or segment).

    7.4 Adaptation and system design

    Principle 2 (welcome changing requirements) and principle 9 (technical excellence and good design enhance agility) together say that welcoming change is a property of the design as well as an attitude. Change without chaos needs one entry door (the Product Backlog), a stable Sprint Goal, and a design that keeps typical changes small.

    Technical debt (Cunningham, 1992): the extra work every future change costs because the code reflects an earlier understanding or a shortcut. The extra work is the interest; refactoring removes it. Cunningham insisted the term was never permission for careless code. Martin Fowler's 🔗 Technical Debt Quadrant (2009) · martinfowler.com: deliberate or inadvertent × prudent or reckless ("ship now, fix after the test" = prudent-deliberate; "no time for tests" = reckless-deliberate; "now we see how it should have been" = prudent-inadvertent; not knowing basic design practice = reckless-inadvertent).

    A component is a named part with one responsibility; an interface is the agreed way it exchanges information. David Parnas (1972; 🔗 Communications of the ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) · doi.org) proposed information hiding: each module hides one design decision likely to change, so a change touches one module. An API (Application Programming Interface) is an interface for programs; API-first means designing and agreeing the interface before the implementation, as opposed to code-first (🔗 OpenAPI Initiative · openapis.org; the OpenAPI Specification is written in JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) or YAML (YAML Ain't Markup Language)). A monolith is one deployable unit; microservices are many independently deployable services (🔗 Lewis & Fowler, 2014 · martinfowler.com); Fowler advises 🔗 Monolith First (2015) · martinfowler.com. The research of DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment): a 🔗 loosely coupled architecture · dora.dev lets small teams change and deploy safely and independently.

    Design → flow. Coupled design → large cards → long cycle times → people start other cards → WIP (Work In Progress) rises → with Little's Law, longer cycle and lead times for everything. Team Menu's café request: old design, one 13-day card (café usable on day 13, average cycle 7.7 days, average lead 9.0, 23 pair-days of work); current design, two 3-day cards (usable on day 3, average cycle 4.0, average lead 5.5, 16 pair-days). Little's Law holds exactly in both (1.77 ÷ 0.23 = 7.7; 1.78 ÷ 0.44 = 4.0) because each window starts and ends with no card open.

    Case 1 · Two projects, one transit agency

    Covers: Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.2 What is a methodology? ; Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.1 Welcome and the rhythm

    Context. Ligne Bleue, a regional transit agency, runs two projects next year. Project A rewires the electrical system of a bus depot under the provincial electrical code; the drawings are approved and the contractor did the same work on the previous depot. Project B is a trip-planning web page for riders who use wheelchairs: nobody knows which features they will actually use, and the elevator-status data comes from a supplier whose interface is undocumented. The agency's IT (Information Technology) department has a thirty-page "methodology" document with rules, roles (sponsor, analyst, developer), a weekly meeting and templates; it never mentions measuring results or reviewing finished work. Managers earn a bonus for "delivering exactly the plan approved in January". Last month the department replaced its board tool and announced "a new methodology".

    Questions.

    1. Which approach, predictive or adaptive, fits Project A and which fits Project B? Give the deciding variable.
    2. In Project B, a two-week cycle ends and one planned item is unfinished. What flexes, and what happens to the item?
    3. Is the IT department's document a methodology? What exactly is missing?
    4. Use "method → culture → results" to predict the effect of the January-plan bonus on Project B.
    5. The trip planner runs on the team's laptops, but no rider has used it. Is it an innovation in the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) sense?
    6. Was the change of board tool a change of methodology?

    Model answers.

    1. The deciding variable is uncertainty about the problem and the solution. Project A has stable, well-understood requirements and known technology: a predictive (plan-driven) approach is cheap and correct, and late change would be genuinely expensive. Project B has high uncertainty on both sides: an adaptive (change-driven) approach lets riders try a usable piece every cycle and the plan follow what they do. The agency as a whole runs a hybrid.
    2. Time and cost per cycle are fixed; the scope flexes. The unfinished item is written down and carried into the next planning (it returns to the backlog); it is not squeezed into overtime and not hidden.
    3. No. It covers plan, decide and deliver (rules, roles, a meeting, templates) but not learn: nothing is measured, nothing reviewed. A methodology without the learn verb is a procedure. Adding a review with riders and a retrospective at the end of each cycle would complete it.
    4. The bonus rewards conformance to an early plan, so people will defend the plan when new facts contradict it. Repeated by a group, that behaviour becomes the culture, and the culture produces its result: a trip planner that matches the January document rather than the riders' needs, with the gap discovered late.
    5. Not yet. The OECD's Oslo Manual counts a new or improved product as an innovation once it has been made available to potential users. A page that no rider can use has not passed that test.
    6. No. A tool is replaceable inside a methodology; the roles, practices and artifacts did not change, so the methodology did not change.

    Why these answers are right. Chapter 1 ties the choice of approach to uncertainty, defines a methodology by five parts and four verbs, explains culture as rewarded behaviour repeated by a group, and requires availability to users in the OECD definition. 🔗 OECD · Oslo Manual 2018 · 🔗 PMI (Project Management Institute) · pmi.org

    Case 2 · The market-stall pre-order page

    Covers: Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.3 Agile in ten minutes ; Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.1 Welcome and the rhythm

    Context. Four students build a pre-order page for a weekend farmers' market stall. They work in two-week iterations and show their work to five real shoppers at the end of each one. In iteration 1 they produced a 25-slide deck describing the whole ordering feature and called it "Increment 1". In iteration 2 they had a rough working page on which five shoppers reserved real baskets. At the iteration-2 review, shoppers asked for "pay at pickup" (not in the plan) and showed no interest in the "loyalty stamps" feature the team was proud of. One member says: "We are Agile, so we don't need to plan iteration 3; we'll see." On Friday the team holds two meetings: one with the shoppers about the page, one alone about how they worked, where they decide to pair on difficult tasks. During the 80-minute build block, a task is half done when the timer rings, and someone proposes staying forty more minutes.

    Questions.

    1. Was the slide deck an increment? Was the iteration-2 page? Justify with the two required properties.
    2. What should happen to "pay at pickup" and "loyalty stamps"?
    3. Which Friday meeting is the Sprint Review and which is the Sprint Retrospective?
    4. Answer the member who says Agile teams do not plan.
    5. Apply the time-box rule to the build block.
    6. Why are reviews every two weeks cheaper than one review at the end of the season?

    Model answers.

    1. The deck is not an increment: nobody can use it. The iteration-2 page is one: it is usable (shoppers reserved real baskets) and it will be additive, extended and verified with later pieces.
    2. Both go back into the backlog, the single ordered list: "pay at pickup" is added and ordered by its value to shoppers (probably high), "loyalty stamps" moves down. The backlog is a plan that is allowed to learn.
    3. The meeting with shoppers about the page is the Sprint Review (it inspects the product and changes the backlog). The team-only meeting about pairing is the Sprint Retrospective (it inspects the team and its process and picks one improvement).
    4. Agile teams plan continuously and in smaller pieces: at the start of each iteration, at every review, at every daily check. What they refuse is treating a plan made before user contact as knowledge. Iteration 3 needs a planning meeting that starts from the backlog the review just changed.
    5. The block is a time-box: it stops when the time is up. The half-done task is written down and carried into the next block or planning; no overtime.
    6. A feedback loop of two weeks finds a wrong assumption while little is built on it. Barry Boehm's studies showed that the cost of correcting a wrong decision rises steeply the later it is discovered, so a mistake found in week 2 costs far less than the same mistake found in week 12.

    Why these answers are right. Chapter 1 defines the increment as usable and additive, separates the two inspections, describes the backlog as one ordered list that changes after reviews, and grounds short loops in Boehm's cost-of-change observation. 🔗 Manifesto for Agile Software Development · agilemanifesto.org

    Case 3 · An insurer asks for a date on day one

    Covers: Chapter 2 (From Waterfall to Agile: the Manifesto and the mindset) → Sub-chapter 2.1 The problem with big plans

    Context. Nordvale Insurance (fictional) wants a customer claims portal. At the idea stage, the team's single best estimate is 20 weeks. The chief financial officer asks for a signed date and budget today, "so the project is under control". He adds: "Waterfall is proven; Royce invented it and recommended it." Three months later the team learns that the claims data must come from a legacy system through a connector nobody has built.

    Questions.

    1. Using the cone of uncertainty, what is the honest range at the idea stage?
    2. What would the range be, with the same best estimate, once requirements are accepted? After product design? After detailed design?
    3. Name two events that would narrow the cone and one that would not.
    4. Where on the cone does the signed date sit, and what happens to the connector discovery under a sequential plan?
    5. Correct the chief financial officer on two points about Royce.
    6. Why did the sequential life cycle become the default in the industry?
    7. What does the CHAOS report suggest about his idea of "control"?

    Model answers.

    1. ×/÷ 4: from 20 ÷ 4 = 5 weeks to 20 × 4 = 80 weeks.
    2. Requirements accepted (×/÷ 1.5): 20 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 13.3 to 20 × 1.5 = 30 weeks. Product design (×/÷ 1.25): 16 to 25 weeks. Detailed design (×/÷ 1.1): about 18.2 to 22 weeks.
    3. Narrowing: a decision on scope (the first release covers car claims only); a prototype proving the connector works; a user test confirming a feature is wanted. Not narrowing: three more weeks passing with no decision or information. The calendar alone narrows nothing.
    4. The signed date is the commitment point placed at the widest part of the cone, the moment of maximum error. A sequential plan then forbids changing it, so the connector discovery can only be absorbed as a failure of the plan rather than used as information.
    5. The word "waterfall" is not in Royce's 1970 paper; later authors attached it to his drawing. And Royce warned that the single pass "is risky and invites failure", recommending that the sequence be done at least twice with a preliminary version first.
    6. In 1985 the U.S. Department of Defense published DOD-STD-2167 (Department of Defense Standard 2167), widely read as requiring phases closed by approved documents; defence contracts paid for a large share of the world's software, so contractors and then everyone else adopted the sequence.
    7. Edition after edition, the CHAOS report finds that only about one project in three finishes on time, on budget and with the planned scope. A date signed at the idea stage is a promise, not control; control comes from frequent inspection of real progress and re-forecasting.

    Why these answers are right. All numbers come from the cone factors (4, 2, 1.5, 1.25, 1.1) applied to the same best estimate; the historical points are the ones Chapter 2 documents. 🔗 Construx · The Cone of Uncertainty · 🔗 The Standish Group · standishgroup.com · 🔗 U.S. Department of Defense · defense.gov

    Case 4 · A web agency's "Agile" charter

    Covers: Chapter 2 (From Waterfall to Agile: the Manifesto and the mindset) → Sub-chapter 2.2 The four values: over, not instead of ; Chapter 2 (From Waterfall to Agile: the Manifesto and the mindset) → Sub-chapter 2.3 Twelve principles, four themes, and the cycle that carries them

    Context. Studio Lumen, a small web agency, posts this charter on its wall:

    • (a) "We are Agile, so we write no documentation at all."
    • (b) "Clients sign a forty-page specification before kick-off; changes are refused after week one."
    • (c) "Progress is reported to clients as the percentage of the plan completed."
    • (d) "Before every launch, everyone works weekends."
    • (e) "Our architect fixes the complete design before the developers join."
    • (f) "Every two weeks we repeat a cycle that polishes the same mock-ups; nothing is usable before month four."
    • (g) "We talk to the client once a quarter, through a written report."

    Questions.

    1. Which lines misread the four values, and which sentence of the Manifesto proves it?
    2. For lines (b) to (g), name the principle that is broken and its theme.
    3. Rewrite line (a) as a line of a Manifesto map.
    4. Describe line (f) with the words iteration and increment.

    Model answers.

    1. Line (a) misreads Value 2 (working software over comprehensive documentation) as an exclusion. Line (b) applies the right sides of Value 3 (contract negotiation) and Value 4 (following a plan) as exclusions of the left sides. The proof is the closing sentence: while there is value in the items on the right, the items on the left are valued more. "Over" is a priority, not an exclusion.

    Chapter 1 · Welcome to the Age of Agile · Essays 1 to 3

    Essay 1 · Methodology or procedure?

    Covers: Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.2 What is a methodology?

    Question. Define a methodology using its parts and its four verbs. Explain why a document without a learning step is only a procedure, and give one practice that would turn it into a methodology.

    Model answer. A methodology is a documented, repeatable set of principles, roles, practices and artifacts that a group follows to plan, decide, deliver and learn. Documented means a newcomer can read it; repeatable means results become comparable; principles give the reasons behind the rules; roles say who decides what; practices are the regular activities; artifacts are what the work produces and keeps. A document that plans, decides and delivers but never asks what happened afterwards cannot improve: the same mistakes return every cycle, because no result flows back into the rules. That is a procedure. One learning practice changes it: a thirty-minute review at the end of every two-week cycle that compares one measured result, for example defects reported by users, with its target and changes one written rule, with an owner. Replacing the board software would not help, because a tool is not a methodology.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). All parts and the four verbs of the definition; the missing learn verb identified as what makes a procedure; a learning practice with a frequency, a measure and an owner; the tool or method distinction as a bonus.

    Essay 2 · Predictive or adaptive?

    Covers: Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.2 What is a methodology?

    Question. Compare predictive and adaptive approaches on requirements, change and delivery. Name the variable that should decide between them and give one project suited to each.

    Model answer. In a predictive (plan-driven) approach, requirements, schedule and budget are fixed early, change goes through formal change control, and delivery comes mostly at the end. In an adaptive (change-driven) approach, a usable result is delivered in every short cycle, time and cost are fixed per cycle while the scope flexes, and the plan is adjusted from feedback. The deciding variable is uncertainty about the problem and the solution. Rewiring a bus depot to an approved electrical code, by a contractor who did the same job last year, has low uncertainty: a plan made early stays valid, so predictive is right. A trip planner for riders who use wheelchairs, whose real needs and data feeds are unknown, has high uncertainty: every two-week cycle should put something in front of them and learn from what they do. A hybrid combines both. Predictive is not a synonym for wrong; it is wrong where uncertainty is high.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). Three dimensions compared on both sides; uncertainty named as the deciding variable; one justified example for each approach; the hybrid or "predictive is not wrong" point as a bonus. 🔗 PMI (Project Management Institute) · pmi.org

    Essay 3 · Iteration, increment and the feedback loop

    Covers: Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.3 Agile in ten minutes ; Chapter 1 (Welcome to the Age of Agile) → Sub-chapter 1.1 Welcome and the rhythm

    Question. Explain iteration, increment and feedback loop, and why a short feedback loop is the cheapest way to reduce risk.

    Model answer. An iteration is one short, fixed cycle of plan, build, review and adapt; in Scrum it is called a Sprint. An increment is what an iteration produces: usable, because a real person can do something with it, and additive, because it joins what came before and is verified to work with it. A feedback loop is deliver, observe real use, learn and adjust the plan. Barry Boehm's studies showed that the cost of correcting a wrong decision rises steeply the later it is found. A two-week loop exposes a wrong assumption after at most ten working days of work have been built on it; a six-month plan can stack about 120 days on it. On the course's fourteen-week laundry-room page, the adaptive team had seven user contacts and half a week of rework, the predictive team one contact and two weeks of rework. A short loop does not remove planning; it repeats planning when the team knows the most.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). Three precise definitions, including usable and additive; Boehm's rising cost of late correction; a numerical comparison of loop lengths or of the two teams.

    Essay 4 · The cone of uncertainty

    Covers: Chapter 2 (From Waterfall to Agile: the Manifesto and the mindset) → Sub-chapter 2.1 The problem with big plans

    Question. Explain the cone of uncertainty using an idea-stage estimate of 10 weeks, and explain why a sequential plan suffers when its commitment point sits at the widest part of the cone.

    Model answer. Barry Boehm measured, and Steve McConnell popularized, how the honest range of an estimate narrows as a project advances. At the idea stage the range runs from a quarter to four times the eventual actual: 10 weeks means 2.5 to 40 weeks. Once the concept of operation is agreed (×/÷ 2) it is 5 to 20 weeks; after requirements are accepted (×/÷ 1.5) about 6.7 to 15; after product design (×/÷ 1.25) 8 to 12.5; after detailed design (×/÷ 1.1) about 9.1 to 11. The cone narrows only when decisions and information remove variability, never with the calendar alone. A sequential plan fixes budget, date and scope, its commitment point, at the idea stage and then forbids change. Every later discovery is counted as a failure of the plan instead of being used as information. Adaptive teams commit one short cycle at a time and re-forecast as the cone narrows.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). Correct ranges for at least two stages; narrowing by decisions, not time; the commitment point at the widest part; the adaptive alternative. 🔗 Construx · construx.com

    Essay 5 · "Over", not "instead of"

    Covers: Chapter 2 (From Waterfall to Agile: the Manifesto and the mindset) → Sub-chapter 2.2 The four values: over, not instead of

    Question. Explain why the word "over" in the Agile Manifesto expresses a priority. For two of the four values, describe one observable practice that honours the left side while keeping something on the right.

    Model answer. The Manifesto closes by saying that there is value in the items on the right, but the items on the left are valued more. When the two conflict the left wins, and the right is still done. Working software over comprehensive documentation: "we demonstrate the increment to two real users at the end of every iteration, and our Definition of Done includes an updated one-page user guide." The demonstration is evidence; the guide is documentation people need, not documentation produced for completeness. Responding to change over following a plan: "we re-order the backlog after every Review and update the release forecast every Sprint." The forecast is still a plan, re-made at fixed intervals. A team that writes nothing, signs nothing and plans nothing has misread the text. The four right-hand items can be controlled from outside and in advance; the four left-hand items exist between people while the work is done.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). Priority, not exclusion; two practices, each observable and with a frequency; something kept on each right side; the misreading named. 🔗 agilemanifesto.org

    Essay 6 · Iterating and incrementing

    Covers: Chapter 2 (From Waterfall to Agile: the Manifesto and the mindset) → Sub-chapter 2.3 Twelve principles, four themes, and the cycle that carries them

    Question. Explain the difference between iterating and incrementing, why Agile methods need both, and map three of the twelve principles onto Scrum elements.

    Model answer. Iteration describes the cycle; increment describes the usable output. A team that repeats cycles without adding anything usable is iterating without incrementing: it refines a prototype nobody can use. A team that adds pieces without a fixed cycle is incrementing without iterating: it accumulates work with no review point, so it cannot compare progress or re-plan. Agile methods do both: a fixed cycle that ends with something usable, inspected with users. In Scrum, principles 1, 3 and 7 (early and continuous delivery, frequent delivery, working software as the measure of progress) are the Increment of every Sprint. Principle 12 (reflect and adjust at regular intervals) is the Sprint Retrospective. Principles 2 and 4 (welcome changing requirements, business people and developers together) are the Product Owner re-ordering the Product Backlog at the Sprint Review. "Fail fast, learn faster" means finding wrong assumptions while little is built on them.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). Both terms defined and contrasted; why each alone fails; three correct principle-to-Scrum mappings. 🔗 Twelve principles · agilemanifesto.org

    Essay 7 · Why Denning's three laws work only together

    Covers: Chapter 2 (From Waterfall to Agile: the Manifesto and the mindset) → Sub-chapter 2.4 Denning's three laws: Small Team, Customer, Network

    Question. State Stephen Denning's three laws and explain what happens when each one is missing.

    Model answer. The Law of the Small Team: work is done by small, autonomous, cross-functional teams, in short cycles, on small tasks, with continuous feedback from the end user. The Law of the Customer: delivering value to customers is the purpose of the whole organization, a Copernican revolution that puts the customer, not the firm, at the centre. The Law of the Network: the organization works as a transparent network of teams instead of a top-down bureaucracy. Without customer focus, small teams optimize themselves and deliver what is easy rather than what matters. Without small teams, customer focus stays a slogan, because nobody can deliver small usable pieces quickly. Without the network, excellent teams inside an unchanged hierarchy are absorbed or dissolved within two or three years, because every request climbs and descends the pyramid. The pressure behind all three is VUCA (Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, Ambiguity): in a stable world a bureaucracy is efficient; in a VUCA world it is late.

    Essay 8 · Empiricism and the three pillars

    Covers: Chapter 3 (Scrum I: the framework, the pillars, the roles) → Sub-chapter 3.2 Empiricism: the three pillars and the five values, section 2.1 Two ways to keep work under control ; Chapter 3 (Scrum I: the framework, the pillars, the roles) → Sub-chapter 3.2 Empiricism: the three pillars and the five values, section 2.2 The three pillars

    Question. Explain empirical process control and the three pillars in their order, including the Scrum Guide's judgment on inspection without transparency and inspection without adaptation. Support your answer with numbers.

    Model answer. Defined process control fits work where the same inputs give the same outputs: plan once, check at the end. Empirical control fits complex work, where unknown factors make results vary: measure often, compare with the goal, correct. Scrum is empirical. Transparency comes first: the work and the process are visible to those who do it and those who receive it. Inspection follows: artifacts and progress are checked frequently to detect undesirable variances, and the five events are the formal inspection points. Adaptation closes the loop: when something goes outside an acceptable limit agreed in advance, the process or the product is adjusted as soon as possible. Inspection without transparency is misleading and wasteful, because it examines a false picture; inspection without adaptation is pointless, because nothing changes. In the course's allergen example, the team that inspected and adapted weekly let 4 wrong entries reach students in six weeks; the team that checked once, in week 6, let 18 through.

    Essay 9 · Product Owner and Scrum Master

    Covers: Chapter 3 (Scrum I: the framework, the pillars, the roles) → Sub-chapter 3.3 The Product Owner and the Scrum Master, section 3.2 The Product Owner ; Chapter 3 (Scrum I: the framework, the pillars, the roles) → Sub-chapter 3.3 The Product Owner and the Scrum Master, section 3.3 The Scrum Master

    Question. Compare the accountabilities of the Product Owner and the Scrum Master, and describe one anti-pattern for each with its consequence.

    Model answer. The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the Scrum Team's work; value is the effect on users and the organization, not the amount produced. The Product Owner develops and communicates the Product Goal, creates and communicates backlog items, orders them in one sequence that weighs value, size, risk and dependencies, and keeps the backlog transparent.     Essay 10 · Why Scrum Teams stay small

    Covers: Chapter 3 (Scrum I: the framework, the pillars, the roles) → Sub-chapter 3.4 The Developers and the Scrum Team, section 4.4 Small on purpose: typically 10 or fewer

    Question. Explain why Scrum Teams are typically 10 or fewer people, using the communication-path formula and Brooks's law. What does Scrum do when a product needs more people?

    Model answer. Every pair of people is one communication path: n × (n − 1) ÷ 2. Five people have 10 paths, seven have 21, ten have 45 and fifteen have 105. People grow linearly while paths grow with the square, so coordination soon costs more than the extra hands bring. Fred Brooks wrote in The Mythical Man-Month (1975) that adding manpower to a late software project makes it later: newcomers must be trained by the people who were doing the work, and each one adds paths. When a product needs more people, Scrum does not enlarge the team. It creates several Scrum Teams that share one Product Goal, one Product Backlog, one Product Owner and one Definition of Done. Amazon's two-pizza rule says the same in other words: split the team, do not enlarge it.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). The formula with at least two values; Brooks's law and its reason; several teams sharing goal, backlog, Product Owner and Definition of Done. 🔗 ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) Turing Award, Fred Brooks · amturing.acm.org · 🔗 AWS (Amazon Web Services), two-pizza teams · aws.amazon.com

    Covers: Chapter 4 (Scrum II: the events, the artifacts, the Definition of Done) → Sub-chapter 4.4 The Definition of Done, section 4.1 What the Definition of Done is ; Chapter 4 (Scrum II: the events, the artifacts, the Definition of Done) → Sub-chapter 4.4 The Definition of Done, section 4.2 Done, usable, and acceptance criteria

    Question. Explain the Definition of Done, how it differs from acceptance criteria, and what undone work leads to.

    Model answer. The Definition of Done is a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product.     Covers: Chapter 5 (User stories, estimation and velocity) → Sub-chapter 5.3 Acceptance criteria and story points, section 3.2 Relative sizing: points, not hours ; Chapter 5 (User stories, estimation and velocity) → Sub-chapter 5.3 Acceptance criteria and story points, section 3.3 The scale: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 ; Chapter 5 (User stories, estimation and velocity) → Sub-chapter 5.3 Acceptance criteria and story points, section 3.4 Planning Poker

    Question. Explain story points and Planning Poker, including anchoring and the way disagreements are resolved.

    Model answer. Story points measure the relative size of a story, effort, complexity and uncertainty together, against a 1-point reference story. They are not hours, because hours depend on who does the work, people compare sizes better than they measure time, and hours turn into promises. The scale is Fibonacci-like because uncertainty grows with size; the course stops at 8 and splits anything bigger. A velocity in points belongs to one team and is never compared with another team's. In Planning Poker, described by James Grenning in 2002, the Product Owner reads the story and its test, the team asks questions for about thirty seconds, and everyone reveals a card at the same moment. More than one step apart: the lowest and highest voters explain, the team votes again at most twice, and then takes the higher number. A flat hand means "cannot estimate yet" and sends the story to a spike.

    Essay 20 · Velocity and capacity

    Covers: Chapter 5 (User stories, estimation and velocity) → Sub-chapter 5.4 Velocity, capacity, burndown and the release forecast, section 4.1 Velocity ; Chapter 5 (User stories, estimation and velocity) → Sub-chapter 5.4 Velocity, capacity, burndown and the release forecast, section 4.2 Capacity in hours

    Question. Explain the difference between velocity and capacity, why they are never converted into each other, and why velocity is never a target.

    Model answer. Velocity is the sum of the story points of the stories that met the Definition of Done in a Sprint; a partly finished story counts zero. For forecasting, a team averages its last three Sprints: 18, 22 and 20 give 20. Capacity is time: people × focused hours per day × working days, for example 4 × 6 × 9 = 216 hours, minus the events. Velocity records the team's finished work in its own relative unit; capacity measures the calendar available this Sprint. Converting points into hours would bring back the hours the team chose to avoid, and the rate would differ by person and by story. Capacity checks that a plan fits the calendar, holidays and exams included; velocity checks that it fits the team's record. Velocity is never a target or a performance measure: once it is rewarded, estimates inflate and the number stops forecasting anything. It is never compared between teams, because each team has its own reference story.

    Why this answer is right (marking points). Both definitions with formulas; the reason they are not converted; the use of each; never a target, never compared.

    Question. Explain the pull rule and WIP (Work In Progress) limits, and what team members do when a column is full.

    Model answer. On a push system, work is handed on whenever a step finishes, whether or not the next step can take it, so it piles up invisibly in front of the slowest person.     Essay 25 · Little's Law and its conditions

    Covers: Chapter 6 (Kanban and flow: WIP (Work In Progress), lead time, cycle time) → Sub-chapter 6.3 Lead time, cycle time, throughput, Little's Law, section 3.3 Little's Law ; Chapter 6 (Kanban and flow: WIP (Work In Progress), lead time, cycle time) → Sub-chapter 6.3 Lead time, cycle time, throughput, Little's Law, section 3.4 Team Menu's check, and why it does not match

    Question. State Little's Law in flow form, give a numerical example with units, and explain the conditions under which it holds and what to do when they fail.

    Model answer. John D. C. Little proved in 1961 that in a stable queue L = λW. In flow form: average WIP (Work In Progress) = throughput × average cycle time, so average cycle time = average WIP ÷ throughput. A support desk that keeps 15 tickets in progress and finishes 5 a week has an average cycle time of 15 ÷ 5 = 3 weeks; with a daily throughput the answer would be in days, so units must match. Daniel Vacanti lists the conditions: arrivals equal departures on average; every item that enters eventually leaves; WIP is about the same at the start and end of the period; the average age of WIP is neither growing nor shrinking; units are consistent. When a window starts empty and ends with an open item, as in Team Menu's Sprint 1 (prediction 10.3 days, observed 6.7), the gap is the finding to report, with its reason, not something to hide. The practical lesson stands anyway: at the same throughput, less WIP means shorter cycle times.

    Covers: Chapter 7 (Agile in the digital business: products, services and innovation frameworks) → Sub-chapter 7.4 Adaptation and system design, section 4.2 Technical debt ; Chapter 7 (Agile in the digital business: products, services and innovation frameworks) → Sub-chapter 7.4 Adaptation and system design, section 4.4 How a design decision changes WIP (Work In Progress) and lead times

    Question. Explain technical debt and Martin Fowler's quadrant, then show how a coupled design lengthens lead times, using Little's Law.

    Model answer. Ward Cunningham (1992) described technical debt as the extra work every future change costs because the code reflects an earlier understanding or a shortcut; that extra work is the interest, and refactoring repays it. He never meant the term as permission for careless code. Fowler's quadrant (2009) crosses deliberate or inadvertent with prudent or reckless: "ship now, repay next Sprint" is prudent and deliberate; "no time for tests" is reckless and deliberate; "now we see how it should have been" is prudent and inadvertent; not knowing basic design practice is reckless and inadvertent. In a coupled design, a small request touches many components and becomes a large card. Large cards have long cycle times, people start other cards while they wait, and WIP (Work In Progress) rises. With Little's Law, average cycle time = average WIP ÷ throughput, so more WIP at the same throughput means longer cycle and lead times for everything. In the course's café example, the old design needed one 13-day card (average cycle time 7.7 days), the current design two 3-day cards (4.0 days).

    Why this answer is right (marking points). Debt, interest and refactoring; the four cells of the quadrant; the chain from coupled design to large cards, more WIP and Little's Law; a numerical example. 🔗 Ward Cunningham, OOPSLA (Object-Oriented Programming, Systems, Languages & Applications) 1992 · c2.com · 🔗 Fowler, Technical Debt Quadrant · martinfowler.com
