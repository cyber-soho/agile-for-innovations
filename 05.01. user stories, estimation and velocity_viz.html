1.1 · From a Product Backlog item to a user story

Slide 5
Slide 6

Last Monday we defined the Product Backlog with the Scrum Guide's words: "an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product". The Guide also tells us what happens to its items over time:

"Product Backlog refinement is the act of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller more precise items. This is an ongoing activity to add details, such as a description, order, and size." — The Scrum Guide 2020, section "Product Backlog", © Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, CC BY-SA 4.0 (Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0) — scrumguides.org

Three words in that sentence define this hour: a description (Lessons 1 to 3), an order (Lesson 4, with MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time)) and a size (Lessons 3 and 4). The Guide says what refinement must produce; it does not say how to write an item. The most widely used way is the user story, and this is the form Mike Cohn made standard in his book User Stories Applied (2004), building on a practice from XP (Extreme Programming) at the end of the 1990s (Mountain Goat Software; Agile Alliance glossary):

As a [role], I want [goal], so that [benefit].

Each of the three parts has a job and a rule. The role is one real user of the product — in your course, one of the users named in your D2 (Deliverable 2, Product Vision Board). The rule: never "the system", "the team", "the admin" or "everyone", because a story written for nobody in particular is built for nobody in particular. The goal is an action or an ability the user wants — to see today's meals, to hide dishes that contain my allergen — and the rule is that it names what the user does, not the technique that will deliver it: a story that says "a dropdown with six colours" has already decided the design before the conversation that should decide it. The benefit is why the user wants the goal, and the rule is that it must not repeat the goal. "I want a search box so that I can search" says nothing; "I want to find a dish by name so that I do not scroll through the whole week" tells the team what problem it solves — and that is what lets the team propose a cheaper way to the same benefit, if one exists.

Why one sentence? A story is deliberately short. It is not a specification; the specification of the 1990s tried to write everything down in advance and we saw in Class 2 what that produced. A story is a placeholder: it records who, what and why in a form that fits an index card, and leaves the details to be decided at the latest responsible moment, in a conversation. Your D2 (Deliverable 2, Product Vision Board) listed three users and their top needs; a story is one need of one user, written so that anybody on the team recognizes it.

1.2 · The three Cs: Card, Conversation, Confirmation

Slide 7
Slide 8

Ron Jeffries, one of the three founders of XP (Extreme Programming), described in 2001 what a story really consists of (Essential XP: Card, Conversation, Confirmation). The Card is the written sentence — a reminder of a promise to talk, small enough to be moved around a board. The Conversation is the exchange between the Product Owner and the Developers in which the details are discovered: what does "today" mean before the kitchen has entered the meals? what happens on a weekend? are there always three meals? It takes place during refinement, at Sprint Planning and again while the story is built. The Confirmation is the acceptance test: the agreed way to show that the story was implemented correctly — in this course, one line in the form Given · When · Then (Lesson 3).

The order of the three matters. The card is the smallest part; most of the understanding is created in the conversation; the confirmation records what was agreed so that it is not lost. This is why a story is not a specification: a document transfers less understanding than a conversation between the person who needs the feature and the people who build it, and a document cannot answer a question. Mike Cohn's video on writing stories (below) makes the same point: the card is the start of the conversation, not its replacement. For your team this has a practical consequence — a story card with no conversation behind it is an empty promise, and Lab 03 asks you to read every test aloud to a member who did not write it, which is the shortest conversation that exists.

1.3 · Three sizes: epic, story, task

Slide 9

Stories come in three sizes, and the names are common practice, not Scrum rules. An epic is a story too big to finish in one Sprint — it usually hides several benefits and several users, and it is split into stories before it is estimated (Lesson 2 shows how). A story is one benefit for one user, small enough to be finished in well under one Sprint. A task is a work item of the Sprint Backlog plan — the "how" of Class 4, decomposed into work items of one day or less (two hours or less in this course) — that builds part of a story and delivers no benefit on its own. Some teams add "theme" or "feature" between epic and story; Mike Cohn's second video explains why the names matter less than the sizes.

Size What it is Where it lives Team Menu example
Epic A story too big for one Sprint; several benefits hidden inside Product Backlog, until it is split "The kitchen keeps the page right itself"
Story One benefit for one user, finished in well under one Sprint Product Backlog → Sprint Backlog when selected "As the head chef, I want to correct one allergen entry myself, so that the page is right without waiting for students"
Task A work item of two hours or less that builds part of a story Sprint Backlog (the plan) "Second-person check against the recipe cards (1 h)"

Why does the distinction matter for your numbers? Because only stories are estimated and only stories count in the velocity (Lesson 4): an epic is too uncertain to size, and a task is finished without delivering anything a user can use. When Team Menu's item 8 — "kitchen edits an entry in under 2 minutes" — turned out to need a sign-in, a correction page and a log, it was an epic wearing a story's sentence, and that is exactly what Lesson 2's S (Small) question catches.

1.4 · Two bad requirements

Slide 10

Activity 1 gives you two requirements written the old way: "The system shall have a login" and "Add a search bar". Apply the three rules before you rewrite them. The first has no role and no benefit — who needs to sign in, and what do they get that they did not have before? The second is a technique: a search bar is one way to deliver a goal, but the goal is not stated — what is the user trying to find, and why? A rewrite is good when a second person can say who the user is, what they can do afterwards, and what problem it solves, without the word "system" and without naming a screen element.

Terms to learn

Product Backlog refinement
The ongoing act of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller, more precise items — adding a description, an order and a size (Scrum Guide 2020).
User story
A Product Backlog item written from the point of view of the person who wants it: As a [role], I want [goal], so that [benefit].
Role (the who)
One real user of the product — one of the users of your D2 — never "the system", "the team" or "everyone".
Goal (the what)
The action or ability the user wants, stated without the technique that will deliver it.
Benefit (the why)
The reason the user wants the goal; it must not repeat the goal, because it is what lets the team find a cheaper way to the same result.
Card
The written story sentence: a short reminder of a promise to talk, small enough for an index card.
Conversation
The exchange between the Product Owner and the Developers in which the details of a story are discovered — during refinement, at Sprint Planning and while the story is built.
Confirmation
The acceptance test that shows the story was implemented correctly; in this course, one line in the form Given · When · Then.
Epic
A story too big to finish in one Sprint; it hides several benefits and is split into stories before it is estimated.
Task
A work item of the Sprint Backlog plan (two hours or less in this course) that builds part of a story and delivers no benefit on its own.
Specification
A document that tries to describe a product completely in advance; a user story is deliberately not one.

2.1 · Six questions for every story

Slide 15
Slide 16

A story that passes the three rules of Lesson 1 can still be a bad story: it may depend on another one, it may be too big, or nobody may be able to tell when it is finished. Bill Wake published in 2003 a checklist of six properties with the acronym INVEST (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable) — the article is still online at xp123.com. Read each letter as a question, and each "no" as a signal that names its own fix.

Letter The question The fix when the answer is "no"
Independent Can it be built and delivered without waiting for another story? Re-order the backlog, or merge two stories that cannot be separated.
Negotiable Does it leave the how open for the conversation? Delete the technical details; keep the benefit.
Valuable Does a user or a customer gain something from it alone? Rewrite the so that; if you cannot, it is a task — attach it to a story.
Estimable Can the Developers say roughly how big it is? Run a time-boxed research task — a spike — then estimate.
Small Can it be finished in well under one Sprint? Split it (section 2.3).
Testable Can another person decide by looking whether it is done? Write the acceptance test (Lesson 3); if you cannot, the story is too vague.

Two of the six deserve a second look because beginners confuse them. Independent is about delivery, not about code: two stories may share a database table and still be independent if each one can be shown to a user on its own. Valuable is judged from the user's side: "As a developer, I want to refactor the database" fails it as written, not because the work is useless but because the sentence names no benefit for anybody outside the team; the fix is to write the benefit the users get — pages that load in under two seconds, say — and to keep the refactoring as the task that delivers it.

2.2 · The lab rule, and the spike

Slide 17

Lab 03 does not ask for six ✓ on every story; it asks for at most one ✗, with a written reason. The reason is honesty: a backlog where every story passes every check was probably not checked. A single ✗ is a known risk the team has written down — "E ✗: we have never built a phone filter; spike of one hour" — and a known risk is manageable. Two ✗ on the same story mean the story must be rewritten before it is estimated.

The fix for E has a name. A spike is a short, time-boxed piece of research whose only output is knowledge: one hour to try the phone filter on twelve test dishes, then a report on Wednesday. A spike is not a story — it delivers nothing to a user — and it is not counted in the velocity; it exists to make a story estimable. Write it with a time-box and an owner, run it before the story is sized, and then estimate the story with what you learned.

2.3 · Splitting a story that is too big

Slide 18

The most frequent ✗ is S. The rule for splitting is older than the patterns: every slice must still be a story — one benefit for one user, demonstrable on its own. A slice that is "the database part" or "the screen part" is a technical layer, and layers deliver nothing until all of them exist; a good slice is a vertical slice, thin but complete from the user's action to the result. Five patterns cover most cases, and Mike Cohn's SPIDR (Spikes, Paths, Interfaces, Data, Rules) list — explained in his video below and on his site — names the same ideas:

  • By workflow step. The user goes through steps one after the other; deliver the first step alone. The chef signs in; the chef corrects one entry; two stories.
  • By business rule. Each rule the product applies is a slice. Every correction is logged with date and time is a rule, and a story of its own.
  • By data variation. Deliver one kind of data first. Monday's meals before the whole week; English before French.
  • Happy path versus edge cases. The normal case first — three meals — and the exceptions — two or four meals, a closed day — later.
  • By user or role. Serve one user first. The sesame-allergic student before every allergy.

After the split, keep the count that your plan can carry. Lab 03 keeps eight stories: the new slices go to the later list at the bottom of your page, where nothing is thrown away, and the Product Owner orders them with the rest.

3.1 · The confirmation: Given · When · Then

Slide 24
Slide 25

Lesson 1 left the third C — the Confirmation — for now. An acceptance test (also called acceptance criteria) is the agreed way to show that one story was implemented correctly. In this course you write one line per story with three words that Dan North introduced in 2006 with BDD (Behaviour-Driven Development) (Introducing BDD); the same three words are the keywords of Gherkin, the language used by test tools such as Cucumber (reference). You borrow the words, not the tools.

Given [the starting situation], when [one action], then [one observable result].

The quality of the whole line sits in the last word. A result is observable when a person or a tool can see it, count it or measure it without asking the author: three meal names are shown, the word "sesame" appears in the allergen list, no dish with milk is shown among the twelve test dishes, 3 of 3 test users name the icons correctly. "The page works well" and "the filter is fast enough" are not observable — nobody can film "well", and "fast enough" has no number. A number is the surest way to make a then observable; when no number fits, a visible fact is enough.

How does the acceptance test relate to the DoD (Definition of Done) of Class 4? The two lists answer different questions and a story needs both. The acceptance test is written per story and answers does this story do what it is meant to do?; the DoD (Definition of Done) is the same five checks for every story and answers is it finished to our quality standard? — second-person check, two phones, spell-check, shared folder, card moved. Team Menu's story 2 can pass its test (sesame appears on every dish that contains it) and still not be Done (nobody compared the entries with the recipe cards); and it can pass all five checks and still fail its test (every entry checked, but the sesame label missing on one dish). Mike Cohn's video below draws exactly this line.

3.2 · Relative sizing: points, not hours

Slide 26

The second number of a story is its size, and the Scrum Guide gives the rule that matters: "the Developers who will be doing the work are responsible for the sizing" (section "Product Backlog"). It does not say in which unit. Most teams use story points, a unit of relative size that combines three things — the effort the story needs, its complexity, and the uncertainty around it — and that is deliberately not a number of hours.

Why not hours? Three reasons, all of them observations about people rather than opinions. First, hours depend on who does the work: the same story takes Karim four hours and Amira seven, so an estimate in hours is an estimate of a person, not of the story. Second, people are poor at absolute estimates and good at relative ones: asked "how many hours?", a team spreads widely; asked "is this about twice the sesame story?", it agrees quickly. Third, a number of hours becomes a promise the moment a manager reads it, and a promise distorts the next estimate. A point sidesteps all three: it says this story is about three times the reference story, which is true whoever builds it.

Two rules follow. Points are relative to a reference: the smallest story the team understands well is called 1, and everything else is sized against it. And a velocity in points (Lesson 4) is a property of one team: 20 points for Team Menu and 20 points for another team are not the same quantity, so velocities are never compared between teams and never used as a measure of performance. Some teams use T-shirt sizes — XS, S, M, L, XL — as a coarser scale for early sizing; the idea is the same, with fewer steps.

3.3 · The scale: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13

Slide 27

Teams size on a Fibonacci-like scale: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, sometimes 20, 40, 100. The gaps grow for a reason that is measurable: the uncertainty of an estimate grows with its size. A team can tell a 2 from a 3 — both are small, well understood — but it cannot tell a 50 from a 53, because a story of that size hides unknowns worth more than three points. A scale with growing gaps stops the team pretending to a precision it does not have: between 8 and 13 there is nothing to choose, and a story that feels like "11" is either an 8 with a spike or a 13 to be split. In this course the scale stops at 8: anything bigger fails S (Small) and is split.

3.4 · Planning Poker

Slide 28

James Grenning described Planning Poker in 2002 (the paper), and Mike Cohn's company publishes the rules used by most teams (Mountain Goat Software). The protocol exists to defeat one effect that is well documented in the study of judgment: anchoring — once the first person says "five", every later estimate moves towards five. So nobody speaks a number first. The Product Owner reads the story and its test; the Developers ask questions for about thirty seconds; then, at a signal, everybody shows a number at the same moment. If the numbers are within one step of each other on the scale, the team takes the majority value. If they are more than one step apart — a 3 and an 8 — the lowest and the highest voter each explain, in thirty seconds, what they know or fear that the others do not; then the team votes again, at most twice, and takes the higher number if it still disagrees. The conversation is the product of the game: a spread between 3 and 8 almost always means that one person knows about a risk, and the estimate matters less than the risk written down.

In class, and in Lab 03, you play with your fingers — no cards, no app. One, two or three fingers for 1, 2 or 3 points; an open hand for 5; a closed fist for 8; a flat hand turned sideways for "I cannot estimate this yet", which sends the story to a spike. The Product Owner and the Scrum Master vote too when they also build; the Product Owner's knowledge of the business value belongs in the explanation, not in the number — a story is not bigger because it is more wanted.

4.0 · Read-aloud script · 10 minutes

4.1 · Velocity

Slide 33

Velocity is the sum of the story points of the stories that met the DoD (Definition of Done) in a Sprint. The definition has a sharp edge: a story that is partly finished counts zero, whatever its progress, because a half-finished story delivers nothing a user can use and because counting halves destroys the one thing a velocity is for — forecasting. For forecasting, a team uses the average of its last few Sprints, usually three. The class example: three Sprints delivered 18, 22 and 20 points; the velocity is (18 + 22 + 20) ÷ 3 = 20 points per Sprint. With 120 points left in the release scope, the forecast is 120 ÷ 20 = 6 Sprints. When the division is not whole, the result is rounded up: 130 points at 20 per Sprint is 6.5, which means seven Sprints, because a partial Sprint costs a whole Sprint.

Three rules protect the number. A velocity belongs to one team and its own point scale; it is never compared between teams (Lesson 3). It is not a target: a team asked to "raise its velocity" will raise its estimates, and the number stops meaning anything. And it is a forecast, not a promise: the Scrum Guide says the Developers forecast what can be done in a Sprint; it never says "commit to a date". A first Sprint has no history; Lab 03 tells you to use your planned velocity — the points you planned into Sprint 1 — and to replace it with the real velocity when the Sprint ends.

4.2 · Capacity in hours

Slide 34

Capacity is a different number: the amount of work the team can do in a Sprint, counted in hours — people × focused hours per day × working days. The class example: four developers, six focused hours a day (not eight: meetings, mail and interruptions take the rest), nine working days (a ten-day Sprint minus one day for the five events): 4 × 6 × 9 = 216 hours. Team Menu's version from Class 4 is smaller: four students × four hours a week × two weeks, minus three hours of events = 29 hours. Capacity tells the Developers how much time they have at Sprint Planning; velocity tells them how many points they have finished before. The two are not converted into each other — that would turn points back into hours — and a team uses both: capacity to check that the plan fits the calendar, velocity to check that it fits the team's history.

4.3 · The burndown chart

Slide 35
Slide 36

A burndown chart plots the points remaining in the Sprint against the days of the Sprint. Two lines. The ideal line is a straight line from the Sprint total on day 0 to zero on the last day: with 40 points and 10 days, it loses 4 points a day; the value at day d is total × (days − d) ÷ days. The actual line is drawn every day from the board: the total minus the points of the stories that have met the DoD (Definition of Done) — again, a partly finished story has not reduced the line at all. The reading rule is simple: above the ideal line means behind, below means ahead; the Daily Scrum is where "behind" turns into a changed plan.

Two shapes are worth recognizing. A line that stays flat for days and then drops means the team finishes stories one at a time, at the end — usually because too many were started at once; Class 6 gives the cure a name, the WIP (Work In Progress) limit. A line that goes up means scope was added during the Sprint, which the Guide allows only in agreement with the Product Owner and never against the Sprint Goal (Class 4); the chart cannot show why by itself, so a scope change gets a written note on the day it happened. The Scrum Guide names burndowns among the practices that "do not replace the importance of empiricism": the chart forecasts; only an inspection of the real Increment tells you what is Done.

4.4 · MoSCoW: what the first release must contain

Slide 37

Points say how big a story is; they say nothing about which stories the release needs. MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time) comes from the DSDM (Dynamic Systems Development Method) community, whose owner, the Agile Business Consortium, publishes the rules (agilebusiness.org). Each letter has a test question. Must have: if we do not get this, do we cancel the release? Should have: important and painful to leave out, but could users manage one more Sprint without it? Could have: would anyone complain loudly if it were missing? Won't have this time: which story do we deliberately not build now — written down, with a reason. DSDM (Dynamic Systems Development Method) recommends keeping the Must haves to a limited share of the effort, around 60 % as the usual rule of thumb, so that the plan has room when something goes wrong; a release where everything is a Must has no plan, only a list.

4.5 · The release forecast of Lab 03

Slide 38

Put the numbers together and you have the forecast Lab 03 asks for, in five lines. Release scope = the points of all Must and Should stories. Remaining = release scope − the points of the Must and Should stories in Sprint 1. Planned forecast = remaining ÷ planned velocity, rounded up to whole Sprints. Cautious forecast = remaining ÷ (0.7 × planned velocity, rounded down), rounded up — a velocity 30 % lower, which is what the first Sprint of a new team usually turns out to be. Dates: Sprints of two weeks ending on Sundays — October 25, November 8, November 22, December 6, December 20. Write the formula before the numbers, and write one sentence about what would have to be true for the cautious case to happen: that sentence is the risk you are managing.

4.6 · Three words from this week's reading

Slide 40

Chapter 5 of Denning describes Microsoft's move to Agile, and three terms from it belong in your vocabulary now. Technical debt is a shortcut taken now that makes later work slower — Ward Cunningham used the comparison with financial debt in 1992 (c2.com); the hand-typed dish names of Team Menu are an example, and Lab 03's Retrospective asks which shortcut your team took. DevOps (Development + Operations) joins the people who build software and the people who run it, so that software moves from idea to users in small, frequent steps. All three are what makes a two-week Sprint possible at the scale of a company; Class 7 comes back to them as design questions.

Glossary

The glossary repeats the *Terms to learn* of the four lessons, in order, with the lesson in brackets.

Product Backlog refinement (Lesson 1)
The ongoing act of breaking down and further defining Product Backlog items into smaller, more precise items — adding a description, an order and a size (Scrum Guide 2020).
User story (Lesson 1)
A Product Backlog item written from the point of view of the person who wants it: As a [role], I want [goal], so that [benefit].
Role (the who) (Lesson 1)
One real user of the product — one of the users of your D2 — never "the system", "the team" or "everyone".
Goal (the what) (Lesson 1)
The action or ability the user wants, stated without the technique that will deliver it.
Benefit (the why) (Lesson 1)
The reason the user wants the goal; it must not repeat the goal, because it is what lets the team find a cheaper way to the same result.
Card (Lesson 1)
The written story sentence: a short reminder of a promise to talk, small enough for an index card.
Conversation (Lesson 1)
The exchange between the Product Owner and the Developers in which the details of a story are discovered — during refinement, at Sprint Planning and while the story is built.
Confirmation (Lesson 1)
The acceptance test that shows the story was implemented correctly; in this course, one line in the form Given · When · Then.
Epic (Lesson 1)
A story too big to finish in one Sprint; it hides several benefits and is split into stories before it is estimated.
Task (Lesson 1)
A work item of the Sprint Backlog plan (two hours or less in this course) that builds part of a story and delivers no benefit on its own.
Specification (Lesson 1)
A document that tries to describe a product completely in advance; a user story is deliberately not one.
INVEST (Lesson 2)
Bill Wake's six properties of a good story (2003): Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable — six questions, each "no" with its own fix.
Independent (Lesson 2)
The story can be built and delivered without waiting for another story — about delivery, not about shared code.
Negotiable (Lesson 2)
The story leaves the how open for the conversation; it contains no technical decision.
Valuable (Lesson 2)
A user or a customer gains something from the story alone, judged from the user's side.
Estimable (Lesson 2)
The Developers can say roughly how big the story is; when they cannot, a spike comes first.
Small (Lesson 2)
The story can be finished in well under one Sprint; when it cannot, it is split.
Testable (Lesson 2)
Another person can decide by looking whether the story is done; the acceptance test makes it so.
Lab rule (at most one ✗) (Lesson 2)
A story keeps at most one failed INVEST letter, with a written reason; two mean rewrite.
Spike (Lesson 2)
A short, time-boxed research task whose only output is knowledge; it makes a story estimable and is not counted in the velocity.
Vertical slice (Lesson 2)
A thin but complete slice from the user's action to the result; the opposite of a technical layer, which delivers nothing on its own.
Splitting patterns (Lesson 2)
By workflow step, by business rule, by data variation, happy path versus edge cases, by user or role — every slice still a story.
Later list (Lesson 2)
The stories below the eight of Lab 03, kept and ordered by the Product Owner; nothing is thrown away.
Acceptance test (acceptance criteria) (Lesson 3)
The agreed way to show that one story was implemented correctly; written per story, one line in this course.
Given · When · Then (Lesson 3)
Dan North's three words (BDD, 2006): the starting situation, one action, one observable result — also the keywords of Gherkin.
Observable result (Lesson 3)
A result a person or a tool can see, count or measure without asking the author; a number is the surest way to get one.
Definition of Done versus acceptance test (Lesson 3)
The DoD is the same five checks for every story (quality standard); the acceptance test is specific to one story (does it do what it is meant to do); a story needs both.
Story point (Lesson 3)
A unit of relative size — effort, complexity and uncertainty together — never a number of hours.
Reference story (Lesson 3)
The smallest well-understood story, called 1 point by agreement; every other story is sized against it.
Relative sizing (Lesson 3)
Estimating by comparison ("about three times the reference") instead of by absolute quantity (hours), because people compare better than they measure.
Fibonacci-like scale (Lesson 3)
1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 (20, 40, 100): the gaps grow because the uncertainty of an estimate grows with its size; this course stops at 8.
T-shirt sizing (Lesson 3)
A coarser relative scale — XS, S, M, L, XL — used by some teams for early sizing.
Planning Poker (Lesson 3)
James Grenning's estimation protocol (2002): simultaneous reveal, explanations from the extremes, re-vote; it defeats anchoring and produces the conversation.
Anchoring (Lesson 3)
The documented effect by which the first number spoken pulls every later estimate towards it; the reason nobody speaks first in Planning Poker.
Velocity is per team (Lesson 3)
A velocity in points describes one team's own scale; it is never compared between teams or used as a measure of performance.
Velocity (Lesson 4)
The sum of the story points of the stories that met the Definition of Done in a Sprint; partly finished stories count zero; for forecasting, the average of the last three Sprints.
Planned velocity (Lesson 4)
For a first Sprint without history: the points planned into Sprint 1; replaced by the real velocity when the Sprint ends.
Capacity (Lesson 4)
The amount of work the team can do in a Sprint, in hours: people × focused hours per day × working days; a different number from velocity, never converted into it.
Focused hours (Lesson 4)
The part of a working day actually available for Sprint work — about six of eight in the class example, after meetings and interruptions.
Burndown chart (Lesson 4)
Points remaining plotted against the days of the Sprint, with an ideal line and an actual line.
Ideal line (Lesson 4)
The straight line from the Sprint total on day 0 to zero on the last day: remaining at day d = total × (days − d) ÷ days.
Actual line (Lesson 4)
The total minus the points of the stories that met the Definition of Done, drawn every day from the board; above the ideal line = behind, below = ahead.
Scope-change note (Lesson 4)
A written note on the day scope entered or left the Sprint, because the chart cannot show the reason by itself.
MoSCoW (Lesson 4)
Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have this time — the DSDM ranking, each letter with a test question; Must haves at most about 60 % of the points.
Release scope (Lesson 4)
The points of all Must and Should stories — what the first release needs.
Release forecast (Lesson 4)
Remaining points ÷ velocity, rounded up to whole Sprints, with the end date; a forecast, never a promise.
Cautious forecast (Lesson 4)
The same computation with a velocity 30 % lower (planned × 0.7, rounded down), and one sentence on what would make it happen.
Technical debt (Lesson 4)
A shortcut taken now that makes later work slower (Ward Cunningham, 1992).
Continuous Integration (Lesson 4)
Combining work into a shared repository at least daily, with an automated build and tests checking every combination.
DevOps (Lesson 4)
Development and operations working together so that software moves from idea to users in small, frequent steps.
