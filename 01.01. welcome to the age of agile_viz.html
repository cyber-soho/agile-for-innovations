▶ Slide 5

Welcome to DIG 123 – Agile Methodologies for Innovation. Before anything else: attendance is being recorded in Blackboard right now, and it will be recorded at minute zero of every session this semester, and again at the end of the Team Studio. Being in the room is where the marks for taking part are earned, so this is the one habit I ask you to build first.

I spent about twenty-five years in the IT (Information Technology) industry before I came to the classroom. In those years I watched teams with excellent tools fail and teams with very ordinary tools succeed. The difference was almost never the tools. It was the methodology — the agreed way the team planned, decided, built, checked its work and learned from it. That is what this course teaches, and it teaches it in one line: fifteen Mondays, one product.

What the course is. DIG 123 is a three-credit course. It has no prerequisite, and no coding is required for the project you will build — a low-fidelity prototype is enough, although coding is welcome. The syllabus gives four learning outcomes. First, understand the concept and the utility of Agile methodologies in business applications. Second, examine and analyze Design Thinking principles and their application. Third, understand the foundations of Scrum. Fourth, apply all of these work methodologies to the assignments, projects and other work you produce throughout your program. We reach them in that order over the semester: the Agile mindset, then Scrum, then user stories and estimation, then Kanban and flow, then the digital business and its innovation frameworks, then Design Thinking, the Business Model Canvas and case studies. The required text is Denning, S. (2018), The Age of Agile, published by AMACOM (the book-publishing division of the American Management Association, now part of HarperCollins Leadership). ▶ Slide 6

Three words we will use every week, defined precisely.

▶ Slide 7
  • A methodology is a documented, repeatable set of principles, roles, practices and artifacts that a group follows in order to plan, decide, deliver and learn. The next lesson takes this definition apart piece by piece.
  • Agile (with a capital A) names a family of methodologies that deliver work in small, usable pieces, obtain feedback from real users early and often, and change the plan on the basis of that feedback. The word entered management vocabulary with the Manifesto for Agile Software Development, signed in February 2001 by seventeen practitioners; you will read it next week.
  • Innovation, in the definition adopted by the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) in its Oslo Manual, is a new or improved product or process that differs significantly from what existed before and that has been made available to potential users. The last clause is the important one for us: an idea that never reaches a user is not yet an innovation. This is why your project will be built for users you can actually reach.
▶ Slide 8

How every Monday runs — the 1:2:2 rhythm. Each week has three parts of fixed length: one hour of lecture, two hours of Team Studio in class, and two hours of Home Sprint at home. The rhythm never changes, so that by Class 3 you can run the studio yourselves and I can coach.

▶ Slide 9

The lecture hour starts with a two-minute check-in. Then it is cut into four short lessons, which the schedule calls beats: about ten minutes of me, followed by five minutes of you. The five minutes are always an activity that earns in-class activity points: a pair exercise, a drawing, flashcards, a fun-quiz on your phone, or a zen-break. Attention is a limited resource. No lesson runs longer than fifteen minutes, so your attention never has to stretch further than it can; in Class 6 you will meet the same principle applied to work itself, under the name WIP (Work In Progress) limit.

▶ Slide 10

The Team Studio is a mini-sprint, every single week: ten minutes of planning, about eighty minutes of building on your product, twenty minutes of Sprint Review — you show your work to another team or to the class and collect feedback — and ten minutes of Retrospective, where the team inspects how it worked and picks one thing to change. Roles rotate: before the Mid-Term every member of your team will have been Product Owner once and Scrum Master once. Those two roles are defined in Class 3.

▶ Slide 11

The Home Sprint is the two hours between Monday and Sunday: finish what the studio started and polish it into the week's deliverable; do the reading — one Denning chapter or a free guide most weeks; and write your individual Learning Log, five lines: what I did, what I learned, what surprised me, one question, one thing to apply next week. Everything is submitted on Blackboard by Sunday 23:59. Keep your own copy of every team artifact — you will understand why in Lesson 4.

▶ Slide 12

The time-box. Every block I just described is a time-box: a fixed, maximum duration allocated to an activity in advance. When the time is up, the activity stops, whether or not the work is complete. What is not finished is not hidden and not squeezed into overtime; it is written down and carried into the next planning. Time-boxes exist because of two well-documented facts about work. First, without a limit an activity expands to fill the time available — the observation C. Northcote Parkinson published in 1955. Second, a fixed cadence makes progress measurable: you can compare what was finished this week with what was finished last week, and that comparison is the raw material of every improvement your team will make. In Scrum, which we start in Class 3, every event is a time-box: the Sprint itself is one month or less, the Daily Scrum is fifteen minutes. You will feel time-boxes today. When the timer on the screen rings, I stop — if necessary in the middle of a sentence — and we move to the activity. That is not rudeness; it is the lesson.

Real-life example · One Monday, time-boxed — Team Locker's first class ▶ Slide 15

Real-life example for beginners

Task of the example. A four-student team — call it Team Locker, because its product idea is a booking page for the campus storage lockers — arrives at its first Monday. The example lays the whole day out as a sequence of time-boxes: the lecture hour (check-in, four lessons, four activities), the Team Studio (planning, build, Sprint Review, Retrospective) and the Home Sprint (finish D1 (Deliverable 1), the reading, the Learning Log, the Blackboard submission). Each block holds planned work; the clock advances minute by minute; when a block ends, whatever is unfinished is not stretched into overtime — it is carried into the next block, and at the end of the Home Sprint into next week's backlog (the written list of work still to do).

How it relates to this lesson. The example is the 1:2:2 rhythm and the definition of a time-box, made visible: the fixed length of each block, the stop when the time runs out, and the carry-over of unfinished work instead of hidden overtime.

Where it returns in the labs. In today's Team Studio, your working agreements for D1 — when you meet, for how long, and what happens when a block ends before the work does — are exactly the decisions this example simulates. In Class 4 the same mechanism becomes the Sprint's time-box and the 15-minute Daily Scrum of your Sprint 1 Kit (D4, Deliverable 4), and in Class 6 the carried-over items become the WIP (Work In Progress) you will measure in your Flow Pack (D6, Deliverable 6).

Returns in the labs: D1 working agreements · D4 Sprint 1 Kit (time-boxed events) · D6 Flow Pack (carried-over work = WIP)

Code — the example as a self-contained HTML page you can run, pause and copy

What the code does. It is a self-contained HTML (HyperText Markup Language) page with a small JS (JavaScript) simulation. A list of time-boxes describes Team Locker's Monday. A simulated clock advances one minute per tick; each minute is spent on the current work item of the current block. When a block's time is up, the block closes: finished items are marked done, unfinished items are carried to the next block. Every event is written to an execution log, and a bar drawing — one segment per time-box — shows where the day stands. Start / Pause freezes the clock so a step can be discussed; Next step jumps to the next event while paused; Restart resets the day; Copy this code copies the whole page; Download visualization saves the drawing as an SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics) file.

How it relates to the lesson. The three parts of the rhythm (60 + 120 + 120 minutes), the four lessons and their activities, the studio time-boxes and the Home Sprint are the data of the program; the "close the block on time, carry the rest over" rule is its logic.

Step-by-step: what the program does, in order

  1. The data section lists Team Locker's Monday as 14 time-boxes. Each has a fixed length in minutes and the work items planned inside it. Two blocks are deliberately over-planned: Build (90 minutes of items in an 80-minute box) and Home Sprint (115 minutes of items in a 120-minute box, before carried-over work arrives).
  2. The state variables remember where the simulation is: the simulated clock, which block is open, the queue of unfinished items, the list of finished ones, and whether the clock is running.
  3. openBox() opens the next block: its planned items are added after anything carried over, so carried work is finished first. The log announces the block and its time-box.
  4. advanceMinute() is the heart of the program. One minute is spent on the first item in the queue; if the item reaches zero it is marked done. The clock ticks. If the clock has reached the end of the open block, the block closes on time: unfinished items are listed as carried over and the next block opens. When the last block closes, whatever is left goes to next week's backlog — never into overtime.
  5. tick() is called by a timer every 60 ms while running; stepOnce() pauses and then advances minute by minute until the next logged event, which is what makes the Next step button useful in class.
  6. toggle(), pause() and restart() implement Start / Pause and Restart: one button that flips between starting and pausing the timer, and one that resets every state variable.
  7. draw() rebuilds the visualization after each minute: one rectangle per time-box (finished blocks in the colour of their part of the day, the open block in amber, future blocks in sand), a rust now marker at the current minute, and the table of items with their status.
  8. copySource() copies the page's own source (with a fallback for older browsers), and downloadSVG() turns the drawing into an SVG file — the two buttons every example in this course carries.
  9. The last line, restart(), puts the page in its starting state when it loads, so the first click on Start begins at 00:00.

What is a methodology?

The definition, piece by piece; why organizations need one; how a method shapes a culture and its results; predictive versus adaptive ways of working.

0:15 – 0:2510 min of readingthen 🖍️ Visualization: the spectrum line · 5 min

Lesson notes ▶ Slide 17

▶ Slide 18

In Lesson 1 I gave you a definition and promised to take it apart. Here it is again: a methodology is a documented, repeatable set of principles, roles, practices and artifacts that a group follows in order to plan, decide, deliver and learn. Five parts, five questions.

Documented — it is written down, so that a new member can read it and a team can be held to it. Repeatable — it is used the same way on the next piece of work, which is what makes results comparable. Principles are the reasons behind the rules: "deliver working product frequently" is a principle. Roles say who decides what: who orders the work, who does it, who removes obstacles. Practices are the activities the group performs regularly — a planning meeting, a review, a daily check. Artifacts are the things the work produces and keeps: a plan, a list of requirements, a working product, a report. Plan, decide, deliver, learn are the four verbs every methodology has to cover; if one is missing — most often learn — you have a procedure, not a methodology.

▶ Slide 19

Four neighbouring words are often confused with it, so let us separate them. A method is a single technique, for example a way of estimating effort. A process is an ordered sequence of activities with inputs and outputs. A framework is a deliberately minimal structure — a few roles, a few events, a few artifacts — that leaves the techniques open; the Scrum Guide calls Scrum a framework for exactly this reason. A tool is software or equipment used inside a methodology: a board, a spreadsheet, a chat channel. You can change the tool and keep the methodology; the reverse is far more expensive.

▶ Slide 20

Why does an organization need one? Four measurable reasons. First, consistency: the same inputs treated the same way give a predictable quality of output, which is the basis of any promise made to a customer. Second, coordination: a shared vocabulary and agreed hand-offs let people who do not sit together work on one product; the person who joins in week ten can read the artifacts of week one. Third, learning: because the practice is repeatable, its results can be measured, compared and improved — you cannot improve what you do differently every time. Fourth, accountability: roles make it clear who decided what, which protects people as much as it protects the product.

▶ Slide 21

Method → culture → results. The syllabus says that methodologies "set the tone" of an organization. Here is the mechanism, without any poetry. A methodology decides which behaviours are rewarded and which are penalized. If the methodology rewards following the original plan, people will defend the plan even when new facts contradict it. If it rewards showing working product to users every two weeks, people will talk to users. Repeated behaviours, shared by a group, are what we call a culture — the set of assumptions and habits a group no longer questions. And a culture produces its results as reliably as a machine produces its output. This is why the methodology is not a detail of the engineering department: it determines what the employees experience every day, what the customer receives and when, and what the stakeholders — everyone with an interest in the outcome: owners, managers, regulators, partners — are told and when they are told it. Consider a hotel group that adds online check-in to its application. Under one methodology the front-desk staff are consulted after the launch; under another they try a rough version in week two. Same product, same people, different method — and a different result.

▶ Slide 22

Predictive versus adaptive. Every methodology sits somewhere between two ends of one axis. The PMI (Project Management Institute) names them in its Agile Practice Guide.

A predictive approach, also called plan-driven, fixes the requirements — the documented statements of what the product must do — together with the schedule and the budget as early as possible, then executes the plan. Change is handled through change control: a formal request, an impact analysis, an approval. Delivery happens mostly at the end. The best-known predictive model is the Waterfall model: requirements, then design, then implementation, then verification, then maintenance, each phase completed before the next starts. It was described by Winston Royce in a 1970 paper on managing large software systems — and it is worth knowing that Royce himself argued in that same paper that a single pass through the phases was risky and that the design needed to be tried and revised at least once. A predictive approach is the right choice when the requirements are stable and well understood and the technology is known: a bridge, a regulatory filing, the wiring of a building. There, early certainty is real, and a change late in the work is genuinely expensive.

▶ Slide 23

An adaptive approach, also called change-driven, assumes the opposite: the requirements will change, because neither the users nor the team can know everything at the start. Work is delivered in short cycles; a usable result appears at the end of each cycle; users see it; the plan for the next cycle is adjusted. Here time and cost are fixed per cycle and the scope — the set of things the product will do — is what flexes. Agile methodologies, including Scrum and Kanban, are adaptive. A hybrid approach combines the two, for example a predictive plan for the building and an adaptive one for the software inside it.

▶ Slide 24

What decides the right choice is uncertainty — how much is unknown about the problem and about the solution. When uncertainty is low, planning early is cheap and correct. When uncertainty is high, an early plan is a guess written in confident language, and every week spent executing it before the first user sees anything is a week of risk. Next week you will meet the cone of uncertainty, the measured fact that estimates made at the start of a project can be off by a factor of four in either direction and narrow only as the work reveals what is true. Digital products for real users are almost always high-uncertainty work — which is why this course lives on the adaptive side of the axis, and why, in the studio, the first thing your team will do with its idea is find the smallest thing a real user could try.

One caution before the activity: predictive is not a synonym for wrong. A team that chooses its method deliberately, for the uncertainty it actually faces, is already behaving well. A team that uses one method for everything, without asking, is not.

Real-life example · Two teams, one product, fourteen weeks — the laundry-room page ▶ Slide 26

Real-life example for beginners

Task of the example. Two four-student teams are given the same brief: a web page that shows, in real time, which washing machines in a student residence's laundry room are free. The users are the 300 residents. Team P works predictively: three weeks of requirements, two of design, five of building, one of testing, then release — the residents see the page for the first time at the release. Team A works adaptively: two-week iterations (fixed cycles of plan → build → review → adapt), and at the end of each one a usable increment — first a static list of the machines, then live status, then a notification when a machine frees up — is tried by five residents. In week six the residence installs new machines with a different API (Application Programming Interface — the defined way one program requests data from another). Team P's design documents and part of its code are now wrong and must be reworked; Team A re-orders its plan and builds the adapter in its next iteration. The example counts, for each team, the number of times real users saw the product, the week of the first usable result, and the weeks lost to rework.

Lesson notes ▶ Slide 28

▶ Slide 29

Now the word itself. Agile, in this course, is a family of adaptive methodologies built on three mechanisms: work is delivered in small, usable pieces; real users see those pieces early and often; and the plan is changed on the basis of what those users do and say. The Manifesto for Agile Software Development, written in February 2001 at a meeting in Snowbird, Utah, by seventeen practitioners, gave the movement its name, its four values and its twelve principles. You will read all of it next week — it is one page, and it is on the Manifesto's own website. Today we need only the mechanisms, and the vocabulary that goes with them.

▶ Slide 30

Iteration. An iteration is one short, fixed cycle of four steps: plan a little, build, review with users, adapt the plan. Its length is a time-box — in most teams two weeks, never more than a month. Because the cycle repeats, the team gets many chances to be wrong cheaply instead of one chance to be wrong expensively. In Scrum, the framework we start in Class 3, the iteration is called a Sprint.

▶ Slide 31

Increment. An increment is the usable result produced by an iteration. Two words in that sentence carry the weight. Usable: a real person can do something with it — read the list, place the order, book the locker. A slide deck about a feature is not an increment; a rough but working version of the feature is. Additive: each increment is added to all the previous ones and verified to work with them, so the product grows week by week and is, at every moment, in a state that could be shown. Your team's project will grow exactly like this: D1 (Deliverable 1) is the first increment of your Portfolio, D2 (Deliverable 2) the second, and so on.

▶ Slide 32

Feedback loop. The value of an increment is not only what it does; it is what it teaches. A feedback loop is the sequence deliver → observe real use → learn → adjust the plan. The shorter the loop, the cheaper the lesson. Barry Boehm's studies of software projects in the 1970s and 1980s established that the cost of correcting a wrong decision rises steeply the later it is discovered — a requirement misunderstood in week one and found in week twelve can cost many times more to fix than the same misunderstanding found in week two. Early feedback is not a courtesy to the user; it is the cheapest risk reduction a team can buy.

Backlog. Where does the plan live? In the backlog: the single, ordered list of everything the product may need, kept in order of value so that the most valuable item is always at the top. Each iteration takes items from the top; each review may add items, remove items or change their order. The backlog is never finished — it is a plan that is allowed to learn.

▶ Slide 33

Review and retrospective. Every iteration ends with two inspections, and they are different things. The Sprint Review inspects the product: the increment is shown to users and stakeholders, and what they say changes the backlog. The Sprint Retrospective inspects the team and its process: what worked, what did not, and one thing to change in the next iteration. You practise both every Monday in the Team Studio, on your own product.

▶ Slide 34

What a Sprint looks like for your team. Take a two-week Sprint of a student team building a locker-booking page. Monday of week one: a thirty-minute planning meeting picks three backlog items and writes one sentence — the Sprint Goal — that says what the increment must let a user do ("a student can see which lockers are free and reserve one"). The two weeks: the team builds, and every day exchanges a three-line message — done yesterday, planned today, blocked by. Friday of week two: five students from another program try the page for ten minutes while the team watches and takes notes; that is the review. Thirty minutes later the team sits alone and answers two questions — what slowed us down, what will we change — that is the retrospective. Monday of week three: the next planning starts from a backlog that the review has changed. Nothing in this cycle needs a manager's permission, and nothing in it waits for the end of the semester.

Now the misconception I hear most often: "Agile means no planning." False. Agile teams plan continuously and in smaller pieces — at the start of every iteration, at every review, at every daily check. What they refuse to do is pretend that a plan made before the first user contact is knowledge. Planning is not reduced; it is redistributed to the moments when the team knows the most.

▶ Slide 35

Denning's three laws. Our textbook's author, Stephen Denning, studied organizations that had adopted Agile far beyond software teams — a bank, a telecommunications manufacturer, a health-information company, a game studio, a global software firm — and asked what they had in common. He found three principles, which he calls laws, and which structure Chapters 2, 3 and 4 of The Age of Agile. Today I only name them; next week each gets its own ten minutes.

  • The Law of the Small Team. Large and difficult problems are broken into small batches of work and given to small, autonomous, cross-functional teams that work in short cycles. Small means about ten people or fewer; cross-functional means the team contains every skill needed to finish an increment without waiting for another department; autonomous means the team decides how to do its work.
  • The Law of the Customer. Every decision — what to build, in what order, what "done" means — is measured against the value it creates for the customer. In Denning's account, the shift of power from the seller to the buyer is the reason the other two laws became necessary.
  • The Law of the Network. The organization operates as a network of teams that share information and coordinate directly, instead of a hierarchy in which information travels up to a manager and back down. In a network, the answer is one conversation away.
▶ Slide 36

You are already inside all three. Your team of four is a small team. The users you can reach — students, a club, a small business, your family — are your customer. And the class, with teams reviewing each other every Monday, is the network. That is the meaning of "learn Agile by living Agile": the course is not a description of the method; it is an instance of it.

Two more facts to place Agile correctly before the quiz. First, Agile methodologies were born in software, but the mechanisms are general: any work where the requirements are uncertain and the cost of change is high benefits from short loops — marketing campaigns, hospitality services, curricula, research. Denning's book is largely about such cases. Second, Agile is not one method but a family: Scrum (roles, events and artifacts in Sprints), Kanban (visualize work, limit work in progress, manage flow), Lean and DevOps are all members, and Box 1-2 of Chapter 1 gives you one-line definitions of each. We will practise Scrum and Kanban ourselves.

Real-life example · Five Sprints for the café pre-order page ▶ Slide 38

Real-life example for beginners

Task of the example. A four-student team builds an online pre-order page for the campus café; the users are the forty or so students who buy coffee between classes. The team works in five two-week Sprints. Its backlog holds twelve candidate features, each with a value score (how much users would gain, 1–10) and a size (effort in points). Each Sprint the team takes the most valuable items that fit its capacity of 20 points, builds them into the increment, and shows the increment to five real users in a Sprint Review. The users' feedback changes the backlog: after Sprint 1 they ask for "pay with student card", which did not exist in the plan; after Sprint 2 the "loyalty points" feature the team was proud of turns out to matter little and drops to the bottom, while the allergen filter moves up; after Sprint 3 study groups ask to split the bill. The example counts the value delivered after every Sprint and compares it with a plan that delivers everything at once in week ten.

How it relates to this lesson. It is the iteration → increment → feedback loop → adapted backlog cycle, executed five times, with the Law of the Customer deciding the order of work.

Step-by-step: what the program does, in order

  1. The data section defines the capacity (20 points per Sprint), the number of Sprints (5), the backlog of twelve features with a value and a size each, and a small feedback script: what the five users will cause after Sprint 1 (a new item: pay with student card), Sprint 2 (loyalty points lose value, the allergen filter gains value) and Sprint 3 (another new item: split the bill).
  2. The state tracks the Sprint number, the current phase (0 = Plan, 1 = Build, 2 = Review, 3 = Adapt), the increment delivered so far, the cumulative value after each Sprint, and the items chosen for the current Sprint.
  3. order() implements the ordering rule of the backlog: value to users first, highest on top; for equal value the smaller item comes first. This is the Law of the Customer written as one line of code.
  4. step() runs one phase. Plan walks the ordered backlog and takes items while they fit in 20 points, logging the Sprint Goal. Build moves those items from the backlog into the increment — the additive property. Review records the value now in users' hands and applies the feedback script: items are added, lowered or raised. Adapt re-orders the backlog for the next plan.
  5. After Adapt the Sprint counter advances; the simulation stops after Sprint 5 or when the backlog is empty.
  6. The control functions (tick, stepOnce, toggle, pause, restart) follow the pattern of Examples 1 and 2. restart() restores the backlog from a deep copy taken at load time (INITIAL), so a second run starts from the original plan.
  7. draw() builds three panels: the backlog (first ten items) as teal bars proportional to value; the increment as a rust stack that grows upward, one block per delivered feature; and the cumulative-value line, one dot per review, next to a single sand bar showing the original plan delivered all at once in week ten.
  8. Read the chart at the end: the line rises from Sprint 1, and its final value exceeds the big-bang bar, because feedback added two features the original plan did not contain and pushed the one users did not want to the last Sprint. The bar, delivered once, could not learn.

Step-by-step: what the program does, in order

  1. The data section lists the seven pieces with their due Sundays and a scenario of submission dates: four on time, one a day late, one three days late, one five days late, and one never submitted. A second object records which pieces feed which part (A–D) of the Mid-Term report.
  2. markKept(late) is the on-time rule as a function: 100% when on time, minus 10% per day up to three days, 0% after that.
  3. step() processes one piece per week: it computes the days between the due date and the submission date, applies the rule, decides whether the piece is in the Mid-Term basis (any submitted piece is; a missing piece is not), stores the record and logs a line whose symbol shows the status.
  4. audit() runs after D7, on the date of Class 8: for each part of the Mid-Term it checks whether all the pieces that part needs are in the basis, and prints complete or INCOMPLETE — from memory. It also prints the average share of marks kept and the count of pieces in the basis.
  5. The control functions repeat the pattern of the other examples: Start / Pause advances one week per second, Next step one week per click, Restart returns to September 21.
  6. draw() colours the ladder rungs and the calendar boxes by status — green, amber, rust, grey — and fills the basis gauge with the number of pieces the team holds; it also refreshes the results table.
  7. downloadCSV() writes the results table as comma-separated text so it opens in Excel or any spreadsheet program; downloadSVG() and copySource() are the standard buttons.
  8.
