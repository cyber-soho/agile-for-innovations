The problem with big plans

Where the sequential life cycle comes from, why the estimate made on day one is the worst one you will ever make, and what seventeen practitioners did about it in a ski lodge in February 2001.

0:02 – 0:108 minthen 🖍️ Visualization: two timelines · 5 min

Lesson notes ▶ Slide 4

▶ Slide 5

Last Monday we drew a line on the board from "plan everything first" to "learn as you go". Today we look at the left end of that line closely enough to understand why it fails so often, and we read the document that was written to move whole industries toward the right end.

Where the sequential life cycle comes from. In 1970, Winston W. Royce, an American computer scientist who managed large software projects for the aerospace industry, published a paper called Managing the Development of Large Software Systems. It contains the drawing that most people have in mind when they say Waterfall: seven boxes in a staircase — system requirements, software requirements, analysis, program design, coding, testing, operations — each one feeding the next. The word "waterfall" is not in the paper; it was attached to the drawing by later authors, because the work flows downward from box to box and, as with water, nothing flows back up. ▶ Slide 6Two facts about that paper matter for us. First, Royce wrote that this simple sequence "is risky and invites failure", because testing — the first moment the software is actually exercised — comes at the end, when every earlier decision has already been paid for. Second, he recommended doing the sequence at least twice, with a preliminary version first, so that the second pass could learn from the first. The industry adopted the drawing and ignored the advice. In 1985 the United States Department of Defense published a standard for its software contractors, DOD-STD-2167 (Department of Defense Standard 2167), that was read almost everywhere as a requirement to work in exactly this sequence, with a document approved at the end of each phase. Because the American defence budget paid for a large share of the world's software in those years, the sequence became the default way of working for an entire generation of engineers and managers.

▶ Slide 7

Why the sequence fails: the error in the first estimate. Let me define the central term of this lesson. The cone of uncertainty is a model of how the accuracy of an estimate changes over the life of a project. It was measured first by Barry Boehm in his 1981 book Software Engineering Economics, from data on hundreds of projects, and it was named and popularized by Steve McConnell, whose company Construx maintains the reference description of it today. The model says this: at the moment a project is only an idea — the feasibility stage — the honest estimate of its effort, cost or duration is not a number but a range, and that range runs from one quarter of the eventual actual value to four times the actual value. A sixteen-week project, estimated at the idea stage, can honestly be estimated anywhere between four weeks and sixty-four weeks. ▶ Slide 8Each decision that removes a source of variation narrows the range: once the concept of operation is agreed, the factor is 2 in each direction; once the requirements are written and accepted, 1.5; once the product design is done, 1.25; once the detailed design is done, 1.1; and the range reaches the actual value only when the work is finished. Drawn on a chart with time on the horizontal axis and the estimate on the vertical axis, the two boundaries form a shape that is wide at the left and closes to a point at the right — hence the name.

▶ Slide 9

Two consequences follow, and they are the scientific reason big plans fail. The first: the cone does not narrow by itself, with the passage of time. It narrows only when the team makes decisions and obtains information that remove variability — a decision about scope, a prototype that shows a technology works, a user who confirms that a feature is wanted. A project that plans for six months without learning anything keeps a factor-of-four uncertainty for six months. The second: a sequential life cycle asks for the binding commitment — the budget, the deadline, the signed list of features — at the very left of the cone, at the moment of maximum error, and it then forbids changing that commitment. Every later discovery has to be absorbed as a failure of the plan rather than as information. ▶ Slide 10This is not a moral failing of the people involved; it is a property of where the commitment is placed on the chart.

▶ Slide 11

The consequence is visible in the industry's own statistics. The Standish Group, a research firm in Massachusetts, has published a study called the CHAOS report (the study's name, not an abbreviation) since 1994, built from surveys of thousands of software projects. Edition after edition, only about one project in three finishes on time, on budget and with the planned scope; the others are late, over budget, reduced in scope, or cancelled. The same reports show that projects run with small, adaptive methods succeed more often, and that the larger the project, the worse the numbers become for the sequential approach. You do not need to memorize the percentages; you need to remember the mechanism: commitment at maximum uncertainty, no correction allowed.

▶ Slide 12

February 2001, Snowbird. By the 1990s several groups of practitioners had independently built lighter ways of working: XP (Extreme Programming, Kent Beck), Scrum (Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber, 1995), DSDM (the Dynamic Systems Development Method, from the United Kingdom), FDD (Feature-Driven Development), Crystal (Alistair Cockburn), and Adaptive Software Development (Jim Highsmith). They were called "lightweight methods", a name none of them liked. From February 11 to 13, 2001, seventeen of these people met at the Snowbird ski resort in the Wasatch mountains of Utah. Robert C. Martin had sent the invitations; Alistair Cockburn, Jim Highsmith, Martin Fowler, Ward Cunningham, Ron Jeffries, Jeff Sutherland, Ken Schwaber, Mike Beedle, Arie van Bennekum, Dave Thomas, Andrew Hunt, Jon Kern, Brian Marick, James Grenning, Steve Mellor and Kent Beck were there. They expected to disagree. Instead, in two days, they agreed on a name — Agile, proposed in place of "lightweight" — and on a text of four sentences, which they called the Manifesto for Agile Software Development. The twelve principles that accompany it were finished in the following months by e-mail, and the same group founded the non-profit Agile Alliance later that year to keep the text public. The Manifesto is still online, unchanged, at agilemanifesto.org, in more than sixty languages, with the names of the seventeen authors — and it is Box 1-1 of the Denning chapter you read this week.

▶ Slide 13

Notice what the Manifesto is not. It is not a method: it prescribes no meeting, no role, no document. It is a statement of what the seventeen valued more, written in a way that still keeps what they valued less. That construction — "this over that", not "this instead of that" — is the subject of the next lesson.

Terms to learn from this lesson

Waterfall — a sequential life cycle in which each phase (requirements, design, build, test, release) is completed and approved before the next begins; Royce's 1970 drawing, named by later authors.
Royce (1970) — Winston W. Royce's paper Managing the Development of Large Software Systems: the source of the staircase drawing, and of the warning that a single pass through it "invites failure".
DOD-STD-2167 — the 1985 United States Department of Defense software standard, read as a requirement to work in phases with an approved document at the end of each — the reason the sequence spread.
Cone of uncertainty — a model of estimate accuracy over a project's life: a range of ×/÷ 4 at the idea stage, ×/÷ 2 after the concept of operation, ×/÷ 1.5 after requirements, ×/÷ 1.25 after product design, ×/÷ 1.1 after detailed design, closing to the actual value at the end (Boehm 1981; McConnell / Construx).
Commitment point — the moment a budget, date and scope become binding; a sequential plan places it at the widest part of the cone.
CHAOS report — the Standish Group's study of software project outcomes, published since 1994; about one project in three succeeds on time, budget and scope.
Snowbird, February 11–13, 2001 — the meeting of seventeen practitioners in Utah that produced the Manifesto for Agile Software Development and the word Agile.
Agile Alliance — the non-profit founded in 2001 by the Manifesto's authors to keep the text public and support its practice.

Real-life example · One estimate, two commitments — Team Menu and the cone ▶ Slide 15

Real-life example for beginners

Task of the example. A student team — call it Team Menu, because its product is a page that shows the campus cafeteria's daily menu with allergen information — has to say when its first release will be ready. The example follows the same project along two paths. On the first path the team behaves as a sequential plan requires: on day one it commits to a scope and a date, and it keeps that commitment while the project runs. On the second path the team behaves as an adaptive plan requires: it works in two-week iterations, measures how much it really finished in each one, and publishes a new forecast after every iteration. The project contains two things nobody knew on day one — the cafeteria's allergen data turns out to come from a supplier feed that needs its own connector, and one planned feature turns out to be unnecessary — and the example shows what each path does with those discoveries.

How it relates to this lesson. The example draws the cone of uncertainty while the project runs: at each milestone the honest estimate is a range (×/÷ 4, 2, 1.5, 1.25, 1.1), and the range narrows only when a decision or a measurement removes variability. The sequential commitment sits at the widest part of the cone; the adaptive forecasts sit at points that get narrower every two weeks. The difference between "the plan failed" and "the forecast was updated" is the difference between the two paths.

Where it returns in the labs. In today's Team Studio, the block "Two plans, one product" asks your team to sketch a Waterfall plan with the date of first real-user contact and an Agile plan with its first three increments, and to compare risk and feedback timing — this example is that comparison, computed. The re-forecast after each iteration is the release forecast you will compute for your own product in Class 5 (D5, Deliverable 5: remaining points ÷ velocity), and the "commit late, on measured data" rule is what the Mid-Term's Part B asks you to apply.

Code — the example as a self-contained HTML page you can run, pause and copy

What the code does. It is a self-contained HTML (HyperText Markup Language) page with a small JS (JavaScript) simulation. The project has a true size that the team cannot see (a hidden number of work units, changed by two events during the project). A simulated clock advances one week per tick. At the milestones of a sequential life cycle the program computes the honest estimate range from the cone's factors; on day one the sequential path fixes its commitment; every two weeks the adaptive path measures the units actually finished and publishes a new forecast. Every step is written to an execution log, and a chart draws the cone, the commitment line and the successive forecasts. Start / Pause freezes the clock so a step can be discussed; Next step advances one week while paused; Restart resets the project; Copy this code copies the whole page; Download visualization saves the chart as an SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics) file.

How it relates to the lesson. Boehm's factors are the data of the program; "the range narrows only when variability is removed" and "the sequential commitment is made at maximum uncertainty" are its logic. The two discoveries in the middle of the project are the reason the two paths end differently.

// What the team BELIEVES it finishes per week on day one (its planning assumption), in work units.
const ASSUMED_CAPACITY=12.5;
// What the team ACTUALLY finishes per week once meetings, questions and defects are counted; nobody knows this number on day one.
const ACTUAL_CAPACITY=10;
// The size of the project as everyone believes it to be on day one, in work units.
const SIZE_DAY_ONE=100;
// The last week the chart can show.
const MAX_WEEKS=16;
// Boehm's cone factors: at each milestone the honest range is (current estimate × low) to (current estimate × high); "at" is the week the milestone is reached.
const CONE=[{name:"Feasibility (idea)",at:0,low:0.25,high:4},{name:"Concept of operation",at:2,low:0.5,high:2},{name:"Requirements accepted",at:4,low:0.67,high:1.5},{name:"Product design",at:6,low:0.8,high:1.25},{name:"Detailed design",at:8,low:0.9,high:1.1}];
// The two discoveries nobody knew on day one: the week they happen, the change in work units, and the reason.
const DISCOVERIES=[{week:5,delta:20,text:"the allergen data comes from a supplier feed that needs its own connector"},{week:9,delta:-8,text:"the 'print the menu' feature is dropped — no user asked for it"}];
// The simulated week; 0 means day one, before any work.
let week=0;
// Work units finished so far.
let done=0;
// The true size of the project as it stands now (it changes when a discovery happens).
let trueSize=SIZE_DAY_ONE;
// The sequential path's promise: a number of weeks fixed on day one and never changed.
let commitment=null;
// The adaptive path's forecasts: one point every two weeks, each {week, weeks} (the total duration predicted at that week).
let forecasts=[];
// The history of honest ranges, one per week, used to draw the cone as it is revealed: each {week, low, high}.
let history=[];
// Whether the clock is running, and the timer that drives it.
let running=false,timer=null;
// The log element, found once.
const log=document.getElementById("log");
// Writes one line to the log, prefixed with the week, and scrolls to the bottom.
function say(t){log.textContent+="Week "+String(week).padStart(2," ")+"  "+t+"\n";log.scrollTop=log.scrollHeight;}
// Returns the cone milestone reached by week w (the last one whose "at" is not in the future).
function milestoneAt(w){let m=CONE[0];for(const c of CONE){if(c.at<=w)m=c;}return m;}
// The size known at week w: the day-one size plus every discovery already made.
function knownSize(w){return SIZE_DAY_ONE+DISCOVERIES.filter(d=>d.week<=w).reduce((s,d)=>s+d.delta,0);}
// The best estimate of the total duration at week w: known size ÷ the rate believed at that time (assumed on day one, measured afterwards).
function estimateAt(w){const rate=(w===0)?ASSUMED_CAPACITY:(done/w);return knownSize(w)/rate;}
// The honest range in weeks at week w: the current estimate times the factors of the milestone reached; once finished, the range is the actual value.
function rangeAt(w){const m=milestoneAt(w);const e=estimateAt(w);if(done>=trueSize&&w>0)return {low:w,high:w,name:"finished"};return {low:e*m.low,high:e*m.high,name:m.name};}
// The sequential path commits on day one: the single-number estimate becomes the promise; the log shows how wide the honest range was at that moment.
function commitSequential(){const r=rangeAt(0);commitment=SIZE_DAY_ONE/ASSUMED_CAPACITY;say("SEQUENTIAL path commits: "+SIZE_DAY_ONE+" units ÷ "+ASSUMED_CAPACITY+" assumed units/week = "+commitment+" weeks, scope and date fixed. Honest range at this milestone ("+r.name+"): "+r.low.toFixed(1)+" to "+r.high.toFixed(1)+" weeks.");}
// One simulated week: work is done, discoveries happen, the adaptive path re-forecasts every second week.
function advanceWeek(){
  // Stop when the project is finished or the chart is full.
  if(done>=trueSize||week>=MAX_WEEKS)return false;
  // Next week.
  week+=1;
  // The team finishes what it actually can, never more than what remains.
  done=Math.min(trueSize,done+ACTUAL_CAPACITY);
  // Any discovery scheduled for this week changes the true size and is explained in the log.
  for(const d of DISCOVERIES){if(d.week===week){trueSize+=d.delta;say("DISCOVERY: "+d.text+" ("+(d.delta>0?"+":"")+d.delta+" units). True size is now "+trueSize+" units.");}}
  // The honest range this week, stored for the drawing.
  const r=rangeAt(week);history.push({week:week,low:r.low,high:r.high});
  // Every second week the adaptive path measures its real rate and publishes a new forecast: weeks spent plus units remaining ÷ measured units per week.
  if(week%2===0&&done<trueSize){const rate=done/week;const total=week+(trueSize-done)/rate;forecasts.push({week:week,weeks:total});say("ADAPTIVE path re-forecasts: "+done.toFixed(1)+" of "+trueSize+" units done, measured rate "+rate.toFixed(1)+"/week → finish at week "+total.toFixed(1)+". Honest range now "+r.low.toFixed(1)+"–"+r.high.toFixed(1)+" weeks ("+r.name+").");}
  // An ordinary week is logged briefly.
  else say("work: "+done.toFixed(1)+" of "+trueSize+" units finished; honest range "+r.low.toFixed(1)+"–"+r.high.toFixed(1)+" weeks ("+r.name+").");
  // When the last unit is finished, both paths are judged.
  if(done>=trueSize){forecasts.push({week:week,weeks:week});say("FINISHED at week "+week+". Sequential promise was week "+commitment+" → "+(week>commitment?"missed by "+(week-commitment)+" week(s), announced as a failure of the plan.":"kept.")+" Adaptive path: every forecast since week "+forecasts[0].week+" moved toward "+week+", and each change was announced as information.");}
  // Redraw the chart.
  draw();
  // Continue unless finished.
  return done<trueSize;
// End of the block opened above.
}
// One timer tick: advance a week, and pause if the project has ended.

// Next step: pause the clock and advance exactly one week.
function stepOnce(){pause();advanceWeek();}

  // Geometry: the plot area starts at x=60, y=40; the horizontal axis is weeks 0–20, the vertical axis is 0–40 weeks of estimated duration.
  const x0=60,y0=40,W=640,H=200,maxW=MAX_WEEKS,maxY=40;
  // Converts a week to an x coordinate and a duration to a y coordinate (values above the top are clipped to the top).
  const X=w=>x0+w/maxW*W,Y=v=>y0+H-Math.min(v,maxY)/maxY*H;
  // Title of the chart.
  let s='<text x="10" y="24" font-size="14" font-weight="700">Estimated duration (weeks) as the project advances — cone, promise, forecasts</text>';
  // Horizontal grid lines and labels every 8 weeks of duration.
  for(let v=0;v<=maxY;v+=8){s+='<line x1="'+x0+'" y1="'+Y(v)+'" x2="'+(x0+W)+'" y2="'+Y(v)+'" stroke="#EFE7DA"/><text x="'+(x0-6)+'" y="'+(Y(v)+4)+'" font-size="10" text-anchor="end" fill="#54646A">'+v+'</text>';}
  // Week labels along the bottom every 2 weeks.
  for(let w=0;w<=maxW;w+=2){s+='<text x="'+X(w)+'" y="'+(y0+H+14)+'" font-size="10" text-anchor="middle" fill="#54646A">wk '+w+'</text>';}
  // The cone as revealed so far: the upper boundary through the highs, left to right, then the lower boundary through the lows, right to left, closed as one polygon.
  const top=history.map(h=>X(h.week)+","+Y(h.high)),bottom=history.slice().reverse().map(h=>X(h.week)+","+Y(h.low));
  // The band itself, light teal with a teal edge.
  if(history.length>1)s+='<polygon points="'+top.concat(bottom).join(" ")+'" fill="#E4F1F0" stroke="#0E7C7B" stroke-width="1"/>';
  // The milestone names, written where each milestone begins.
  CONE.forEach(c=>{if(c.at<=week)s+='<text x="'+(X(c.at)+3)+'" y="'+(y0+10+CONE.indexOf(c)*11)+'" font-size="8.5" fill="#0A5F5E">wk '+c.at+': '+c.name+'</text>';});
  // The sequential commitment: a rust horizontal line at the promised duration, labelled.

Step-by-step: what the program does, in order

  1. The data section defines the project: its size as believed on day one (100 work units), the capacity the team assumes (12.5 units per week) and the capacity it actually achieves once meetings, questions and defects are counted (10 units per week — a number nobody knows on day one), the two discoveries (week 5: the allergen feed needs a connector, +20 units; week 9: the "print the menu" feature is dropped, −8 units), and Boehm's cone factors for the five milestones.
  2. The state variables remember the simulated week, the units finished so far, the current true size, the sequential commitment (fixed once, on day one), the list of adaptive forecasts, and the history of honest ranges used to draw the cone.
  3. knownSize(), estimateAt() and rangeAt() compute, for a given week, what the team knows (day-one size plus the discoveries made so far), its best estimate (known size ÷ the rate believed at that time — assumed on day one, measured afterwards) and the honest range: that estimate times the low and high factors of the milestone reached. That is the cone, computed week by week.
  4. commitSequential() runs once, at week 0: it takes the single-number estimate of day one (100 units ÷ 12.5 assumed units per week = 8 weeks) and freezes it as the promised date, while the log prints the honest range at that moment (2 to 32 weeks).
  5. advanceWeek() is the heart of the program. The team finishes what it actually can; if a discovery is scheduled for this week the true size changes and the log explains why; the honest range of the week is stored; at the end of every second week the adaptive path computes a new forecast — weeks spent plus units remaining ÷ measured weekly rate — and stores it as a point on the chart. When the last unit is finished (week 12), the log judges both paths: the promise of week 8 was missed by four weeks and is announced as a failure of the plan; the forecasts moved to 12 in steps, each announced as information.
  6. draw() rebuilds the chart: the cone as a shaded band revealed week by week and narrowing as milestones are reached, the sequential commitment as a dashed rust line at 8 weeks, the adaptive forecasts as teal dots joined by a line, and a vertical marker at the current week. The status line under the chart gives the honest range now and the two paths' numbers.
  7. tick() is called by a timer every 700 ms while running; stepOnce() pauses and advances exactly one week, which is what makes Next step useful in class.
  8. toggle(), pause() and restart() implement Start / Pause and Restart: one button that flips between starting and pausing the timer, and one that resets every state variable.
  9. copySource() copies the page's own source and downloadSVG() turns the chart into an SVG file — the two buttons every example in this course carries. The last line, restart(), puts the page in its starting state when it loads.

The four values — "over", not "instead of"

The four sentences of the Manifesto read one by one: what each left side means, why each right side still has value, and how to recognize a value in a team's behaviour.

0:15 – 0:2510 minthen 🗂️ Flashcards ×4, call and response · 5 min

Lesson notes ▶ Slide 17

▶ Slide 18

Here is the text the seventeen agreed on. I will read it exactly as it stands on agilemanifesto.org, because every word of its structure was argued over.

We are uncovering better ways of developing software by doing it and helping others do it. Through this work we have come to value:

Individuals and interactions over processes and tools

Working software over comprehensive documentation

Customer collaboration over contract negotiation

Responding to change over following a plan

That is, while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more.

— © 2001, the seventeen authors listed at agilemanifesto.org. This declaration may be freely copied in any form, but only in its entirety through this notice.

▶ Slide 19

The grammar first. Each of the four middle sentences has the same shape: a left side, the word over, a right side. The last sentence tells you how to read that word. "Over" expresses a priority: when the two sides conflict — when there is time for only one, money for only one, attention for only one — the left side wins. It does not express an exclusion: the right side keeps its value and is still done. A team that produces no documentation at all, signs no contract and makes no plan has not applied the Manifesto; it has misread it. This is the most common misreading in the industry, and the reason the last sentence exists. Keep the two words apart for the rest of the semester: priority is what the Manifesto asks for; exclusion is the error.

▶ Slide 20

Value 1 — Individuals and interactions over processes and tools. The left side: the people doing the work, and the direct exchanges between them — a conversation, a question answered at the next desk, a decision taken together. The right side: the defined procedures a team follows and the software it uses to follow them — a ticketing system, a workflow with approval steps, a template. Why the left is valued more: in knowledge work, the quality of the result depends on the quality of the understanding between the people, and understanding is produced by interaction, not by procedure. A process cannot notice that two people mean different things by the same word; a five-minute conversation can. Why the right still has value: a process makes work repeatable and a tool keeps a record that survives the conversation. What it looks like in practice: when the tool and the conversation disagree, the team fixes the understanding first and updates the tool after — not the reverse. Your team will feel this value every time you decide something on your board that you did not actually discuss.

▶ Slide 21

Value 2 — Working software over comprehensive documentation. The left side: a product that a user can actually operate, however small. The right side: complete written descriptions of what the product will do, how it is built and how it is used. Why the left is valued more: working software is evidence; a document is a prediction. A document can describe a feature that will never work, will never be wanted, or will be understood differently by everyone who reads it; a working feature in front of a user settles all three questions at once. Why the right still has value: documentation that is needed — the acceptance criteria of a feature, an explanation of an interface, the instructions a new team member needs — should be written, and written well. The Manifesto's own word is comprehensive: it objects to documentation produced for its own completeness, not to documentation. In this course the rule takes a concrete form from Class 4 on, in the Definition of Done: "done" means usable, and the documentation that makes it usable is part of done.

▶ Slide 22

Value 3 — Customer collaboration over contract negotiation. The left side: working with the person who will use or pay for the product, continuously, as the product takes shape. The right side: agreeing in advance, in a binding document, exactly what will be delivered, when and for how much — and then holding each side to the letter of that document. Why the left is valued more: Lesson 1 gave the reason. At the moment a contract is negotiated, both parties are at the widest part of the cone of uncertainty; the document fixes their shared ignorance. Collaboration lets the two parties use what they learn. Why the right still has value: a contract protects both parties from bad faith and gives each of them a basis for planning; Agile contracts exist — they fix the budget and the cadence and leave the detailed scope open. What it looks like in practice: the customer, or someone who represents the customer, attends the review of every iteration and can change the order of the remaining work. In Scrum, from Class 3, that person has a name: the Product Owner.

▶ Slide 23

Value 4 — Responding to change over following a plan. The left side: treating a change in requirements, technology or market as information, and adjusting the work to it. The right side: executing the plan as it was approved, and measuring success as conformance to that plan. Why the left is valued more: a plan is a hypothesis made under uncertainty; when reality contradicts it, following it produces a product that conforms to the plan and not to the need. Why the right still has value: without a plan there is nothing to adapt, nobody outside the team can coordinate with it, and there is no way to see that a change is a change. Agile teams plan constantly — a Sprint has a plan, a release has a plan — but they plan in short horizons, and they re-plan at fixed intervals on the basis of what was actually finished. The plan is the instrument; the response is the purpose.

▶ Slide 24

Reading the four together. Notice that all four right sides are things an organization can inspect and control from the outside — a tool, a document, a contract, a plan — and all four left sides are things that only exist between people while the work is being done. That is not an accident. The seventeen were describing a way of working in which control comes from frequent inspection of real results rather than from documents approved in advance. Denning, whom you read this week, calls that shift a change of mindset rather than a change of method, and in Lesson 4 you will see the three laws he derives from it.

▶ Slide 25

How you will use the four values this week. Today's studio ends with a Manifesto map: for each of the four values, one concrete practice your team commits to. A practice is not a slogan; it is an observable behaviour with a frequency — "we demonstrate the increment to two real users at the end of every iteration", not "we listen to customers". The map goes into D2 (Deliverable 2), and in Class 3 it becomes the basis of your team's values pledge in D3 (Deliverable 3). The example that follows shows how a team classifies its candidate practices under the four values, and why a good map keeps the right side of each value on the scale.

Real-life example · Team Menu's Manifesto map — twelve candidate practices on four scales ▶ Slide 27

Real-life example for beginners

Task of the example. Team Menu — the cafeteria-menu team from Lesson 1 — has to hand in a Manifesto map with D2 (Deliverable 2): for each of the four values, one concrete practice. During its studio the team lists twelve candidate practices, some of which its members believe to be "Agile" and some of which they are unsure about. The example takes the twelve candidates one at a time, decides which of the four values each one concerns, decides whether the practice adds weight to the left side or the right side of that value, and decides whether the team keeps it — a practice that would apply the right side as an exclusion of the left (a signed thirty-page requirements document before any work, a freeze on change requests after week one) is rejected with its reason. The result is drawn as four balance scales, one per value, that tilt as practices are kept; at the end the program prints the map itself: the practice chosen for each value, with the right-side practice that stays on the scale next to it.

How it relates to this lesson. The example is the reading rule of the Manifesto — priority, not exclusion — turned into a decision procedure. A scale that tilts fully to the left with nothing on the right is a warning, not a success: it means the team has excluded documentation, tools, contracts or plans altogether. A well-built map tilts left on all four scales and still carries something on each right side.

Step-by-step: what the program does, in order

  1. The data section lists the four values with their left and right sides, and the twelve candidate practices in the order the team discusses them. Each practice has a value (1 to 4), a side ("L" or "R"), a weight (1 to 3), a keep flag — true if the team puts it on the map, false if it would apply the right side as an exclusion of the left — and a one-line why that the log prints.
  2. The state variables hold the index of the next practice to discuss, the running left and right totals of each scale, the practices kept under each pan, the practices rejected, and whether the run is going.
  3. processNext() takes the next practice. A kept practice adds its weight to the left or right pan of its value and is remembered under that pan; a rejected practice is listed under the scale with a cross and is not weighed. Either way the log prints the classification and its reason. After the sixth practice — the four heavy left-side practices and the first two rejections — the program checks every scale and writes a warning for each right pan that is still empty: "over" is a priority, not an exclusion.
  4. draw() redraws the four scales: a beam whose tilt angle is proportional to (left − right), the left and right pans with their totals, the kept practices listed under each pan, and the rejected ones in rust under the right pan.
  5. When the twelfth practice is discussed, finish() chooses, for every value, the heaviest kept left-side practice and the practice kept on the right, and prints the four rows of the Manifesto map in the log: eight practices kept, four rejected, every scale tilting left with something light on its right pan.
  6. tick() runs processNext() every 1.2 seconds while running; stepOnce() pauses and processes exactly one practice — the button to use when discussing why a practice is kept or rejected.
  7. toggle(), pause() and restart() implement Start / Pause and Restart.
  8. copySource() and downloadSVG() are the two standard buttons; restart() on the last line puts the scales in their empty starting state.

Lesson notes ▶ Slide 29

▶ Slide 30

The four values say what the seventeen valued; the twelve principles say what a team that holds those values does. I will not ask you to memorize twelve numbered sentences. I will ask you to recognize each principle when you see a team behaving according to it — or against it — which is what today's fun-quiz, next week's Checkpoint Quiz and the Mid-Term all test. To make that possible we group the twelve into four themes. The grouping is this course's; the sentences are the Manifesto's, quoted exactly.

▶ Slide 31

Theme 1 — Customer and value. Principle 1: Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software. Observable behaviour: something usable reaches a user in the first weeks, and again at short intervals — not once at the end. Principle 2: Welcome changing requirements, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage. Observable behaviour: a change request is treated as information about what the customer needs, and re-ordered into the work, rather than refused because it is late. Principle 4: Business people and developers must work together daily throughout the project. Observable behaviour: the people who know the need and the people who build the product talk every day, not through a document once a quarter. All three describe the same thing from different sides: the customer's need is the reason the work exists, and the team keeps a continuous connection to it.

▶ Slide 32

Theme 2 — People and interaction. Principle 5: Build projects around motivated individuals. Give them the environment and support they need, and trust them to get the job done. Observable behaviour: the team decides how to do its work; management removes obstacles instead of assigning tasks. Principle 6: The most efficient and effective method of conveying information to and within a development team is face-to-face conversation. Observable behaviour: a question is asked across the table, or on a video call, before it is written in a ticket. Principle 11: The best architectures, requirements, and designs emerge from self-organizing teams. Observable behaviour: design decisions are made by the people who will build and maintain the design, and they are allowed to change as the team learns. Note the word emerge: the design is not fixed in advance, it takes shape as the work proceeds.

▶ Slide 33

Theme 3 — Working product and feedback. Principle 3: Deliver working software frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter timescale. Observable behaviour: a fixed, short delivery interval — in this course, two weeks. Principle 7: Working software is the primary measure of progress. Observable behaviour: the team reports what a user can now do that they could not do before, not a percentage of a plan completed. Principle 12: At regular intervals, the team reflects on how to become more effective, then tunes and adjusts its behavior accordingly. Observable behaviour: a retrospective at the end of every interval, with at least one change actually made before the next one. The three principles form a loop: deliver, measure by what works, reflect, adjust.

▶ Slide 34

Theme 4 — Sustainability and excellence. Principle 8: Agile processes promote sustainable development. The sponsors, developers, and users should be able to maintain a constant pace indefinitely. Observable behaviour: no "crunch"; the amount of work taken into an interval is what the team finished in the previous ones. Principle 9: Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility. Observable behaviour: tests are written, code is reviewed, shortcuts are recorded and paid back — because a product that is hard to change cannot respond to change. Principle 10: Simplicity — the art of maximizing the amount of work not done — is essential. Observable behaviour: the team builds the smallest thing that answers the need and leaves out what nobody asked for.

▶ Slide 35

The mechanism under the principles: iteration and increment. Six of the twelve principles cannot be practised at all without a short, repeating cycle, so let me define the three terms precisely. An iteration is one complete pass through plan → build → verify → review, of fixed length, repeated. The word describes the cycle. An increment is the result that a cycle adds to the product: a usable piece of the product that did not exist before, which is added to everything delivered earlier and verified together with it. The word describes the output. The two words are often confused, so here is the test: a team that repeats a cycle without adding anything usable is iterating without incrementing — it is refining; a team that adds pieces without a fixed cycle is incrementing without iterating — it is accumulating without a review point. Agile methods do both at once: fixed cycles, each ending in a usable, additive increment. A feedback loop, the third term, comes from control engineering: the output of a process is measured, compared with the goal, and the difference is used to correct the next input. In our case the output is the increment, the measurement is a real user trying it, and the correction is a change in the order or content of the remaining work. Without the loop, an iteration is just a schedule.

▶ Slide 36

Why the cycle must be short — "fail fast, learn faster". The slogan is imprecise, so here is its exact content. Every plan contains assumptions that are wrong; the cost of discovering a wrong assumption grows with the amount of work built on top of it. A short cycle with a real review at its end finds the wrong assumption when little has been built on it, and turns the discovery into a change of plan instead of a failure of the plan. The value is not in failing; it is in the low cost of the discovery and the speed of the correction. Lesson 1's cone gave the same result from the other side: information narrows the cone, and a two-week review produces information every two weeks.

▶ Slide 37

The container: the Sprint. In Scrum, which we open next Monday, the iteration has a name and a length: the Sprint, a fixed time-box of one month or less — two weeks in this course — inside which the team plans, builds, reviews the increment with stakeholders and holds its retrospective. Everything in this lesson has a place in it: Principles 1, 3 and 7 are the increment at the Sprint's end; 2 and 4 are the Product Owner in the review; 5, 6 and 11 are the Developers deciding how; 12 is the Retrospective; 8, 9 and 10 are the Definition of Done and the amount of work the team takes on. The example that follows takes one Sprint of Team Menu, day by day, and checks its behaviour against the twelve principles — it is the checklist your team will use in its own retrospectives.

Denning's three laws — Small Team, Customer, Network

What the organizations that made Agile work at scale have in common, in the three laws of the required text, and why the same laws apply to a hotel, a bank, a marketing department and a school.

0:45 – 0:5510 minthen 🧘 Zen-break + one-minute paper · 5 min

Lesson notes ▶ Slide 41

▶ Slide 42

We have read the Manifesto, a text from 2001 written by and for software developers. The required text of this course, Stephen Denning's The Age of Agile (2018), asks a different question: when the same ideas were applied to whole organizations — banks, manufacturers, retailers, a music service, a fighter-aircraft programme — what did the ones that succeeded have in common? Denning is not a software engineer. He is a former director of knowledge management at the World Bank, an author on leadership, and a columnist for Forbes, and he studied Agile from the management side for a decade before writing the book. His answer is three regularities he calls laws — not laws of nature, but conditions he found present in every organization where Agile worked and absent where it failed. Chapters 2, 3 and 4 of the book are named after them; Chapter 2 is your reading for this week.

▶ Slide 43

The Law of the Small Team. Denning's statement: work should in principle be done by small, autonomous, cross-functional teams, working in short cycles on relatively small tasks, and obtaining continuous feedback from the ultimate customer or end user. Every word of that sentence is a design decision, so let me take them apart. Small: few enough that everyone knows what everyone else is doing — Amazon's rule of thumb, which Denning cites, is a team that two pizzas can feed. Autonomous: the team decides how to do the work; it is given a goal, not a task list. Cross-functional: the team contains every skill needed to finish a piece of work without waiting for another department — in a software team a designer, developers and a tester; in a hotel team a front-desk agent, a housekeeping lead and a technician. Short cycles: the iteration of Lesson 3. Relatively small tasks: work cut into pieces that fit inside one cycle. Continuous feedback from the end user: the feedback loop, closed every cycle. ▶ Slide 44The law is called a law because Denning found that when any one of these conditions was removed — a team of forty, a team that had to ask permission for every decision, a team without a tester, a cycle of six months — the results of the other conditions disappeared with it. His main example in Chapter 2 is Menlo Innovations, a software company in Ann Arbor, Michigan, that works in pairs which change every week, in one-week cycles, with clients and users in the room; he uses it because it shows the law operating at its simplest scale.

▶ Slide 45

The Law of the Customer. Denning's statement: the organization is obsessed with delivering value to customers, and this is understood as the purpose of the whole organization, not as a slogan of the sales department. He calls the change behind this law a Copernican revolution in management. The comparison is exact rather than decorative: in the twentieth-century firm, the firm was at the centre and the customer was one of several parties around it — customers, shareholders, employees, regulators; in the Agile organization the customer is at the centre and every part of the firm, including the parts that never meet a customer, is judged by whether it adds value for that customer. Denning traces the idea to Peter Drucker, who wrote in 1954 that the only valid definition of a business's purpose is to create a customer, and he explains why it became unavoidable only recently: globalization, deregulation and the Internet moved market power from the seller to the buyer. A customer who can compare every offer in seconds and switch in one click does not stay with an organization that serves its own processes first. The Manifesto's first principle — satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery — is this law seen from inside one team; the law is the same principle seen from the top of the organization.

▶ Slide 46

The Law of the Network. Denning's statement: the whole organization works as a transparent network of teams that collaborate toward the common goal of delighting the customer, rather than as a top-down bureaucracy in which information rises through layers and decisions descend through them. This law exists because of a failure Denning documents repeatedly: organizations that created excellent small teams and left the rest of the structure unchanged. The teams delivered every two weeks; the layers above them still approved budgets once a year, assigned people by function and measured departments against each other; within two or three years the teams were absorbed or dissolved. The point of the network is not that hierarchy disappears — someone still decides the strategy — but that a request or a piece of information travels directly between the teams that need it, instead of up one chain of command and down another. Denning's examples in Chapter 4 include Spotify, whose small teams — it calls them squads — are grouped in tribes and connected across tribes by chapters and guilds, and Barclays, a bank that reorganized thousands of people into such teams. The measurable sign of a network is short: how many hand-offs, and how many days, between a customer's request and its resolution. You will measure exactly that quantity in Class 6, under the name lead time.

▶ Slide 47

How the three laws fit together. The Small Team is where the work is done; the Customer is why it is done; the Network is how the teams are connected so that the first two survive. Denning is explicit that the three are needed together: small teams without a customer focus optimize themselves; a customer focus without small teams cannot deliver; both without a network are crushed by the bureaucracy around them. He also notes the environment that makes the three necessary, with an acronym you will meet again in your program: VUCA (Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, Ambiguity), a term the United States Army War College coined for the post-Cold-War world. In a stable environment a bureaucracy is efficient; in a VUCA environment it is late.

▶ Slide 48

Agile beyond software. The Manifesto says "software" in every value; the laws say nothing about software at all, and that is the point of Denning's book. Three examples with a document you can read. In marketing, a group of practitioners met in San Francisco in June 2012 and wrote the Agile Marketing Manifesto, modelled on the 2001 text — validated learning over opinions and conventions, many small experiments over a few large bets, adaptive campaigns over big-bang campaigns. In education, a Dutch chemistry teacher, Willy Wijnands, adapted Scrum for secondary-school classes in 2011 under the name eduScrum; teams of students plan, build and review their learning in sprints, with the teacher as Product Owner — a method now used in schools in several countries, and not far from the rhythm of this course. Real-life example for beginners

Task of the example. A forty-room hotel in Montréal — call it Hôtel du Plateau — receives, over one week, six guest requests: the Wi-Fi in room 214 has stopped, a guest asks for a late check-out, a guest with a nut allergy asks what she can eat at breakfast, a bedside lamp is broken, a corporate guest needs an invoice split in two, and a family asks for a crib. The example routes the same six requests through two structures. In the pyramid, a request climbs from the front desk to the duty manager, to the general manager, and where necessary to an external contractor, and the answer climbs back down; every hop is a hand-off with a waiting time, and some hops need an approval. In the network, a small cross-functional guest-experience team — a front-desk agent, a housekeeping lead and a technician, with authority to spend up to two hundred dollars without asking — receives the request directly and resolves it inside the team, escalating only what the team cannot do. For each request the example counts the hand-offs and the elapsed hours, and at the end it compares the average lead time of the two structures.

How it relates to this lesson. The team in the network is the Law of the Small Team (small, autonomous, cross-functional, with the guest's feedback at hand); the guest at the centre of every decision is the Law of the Customer; the two structures side by side are the Law of the Network against the bureaucracy Denning contrasts it with. The lead time counted at the end is the measurable sign of a network the lesson describes.
