Terms to learn

Methodology
A shared way of working that sets how a team plans, decides, delivers and learns (Class 1). (Lesson 1)
Feedback cycles per year
How often an organization can learn from users in one year: 52 weeks ÷ weeks per release — 26 for two-week increments, 1 for a yearly release. (Lesson 1)
Law of the Small Team
Work is done by small, autonomous, cross-functional teams in short cycles, on small tasks, with continuous user feedback (Denning). (Lesson 1)
Law of the Customer
The purpose of the organization is to add value for customers; everyone can see how their own work contributes (Denning). (Lesson 1)
Law of the Network
The organization works as a network of teams toward a common goal, not as a top-down hierarchy (Denning). (Lesson 1)
Conway's Law (1968)
Organizations that design systems produce designs that copy their own communication structures. (Lesson 1)
Two-pizza team rule
Amazon's policy: teams small enough to be fed by two pizzas (under about ten people), each owning one product or service end to end; split them, do not enlarge them. (Lesson 1)
Communication paths
n(n−1)/2 possible pairs in a team of n people: 6 for 4 people, 66 for 12. (Lesson 1)
CI
Integrating every member's work into a shared repository at least daily, checked by an automated build and tests. (Lesson 1)
CD
The ability to get changes of all types to users quickly, safely and sustainably; the product is always releasable. (Lesson 1)
DevOps
The builders and the operators of a product working as one team with shared responsibility for the product in use. (Lesson 1)
Change lead time (DORA)
The time a change takes from being committed to version control to being deployed in production. (Lesson 1)
Product / service
The product is what the user receives; the service is the continuing activity that keeps value reaching the user. (Lesson 1)
Value map
Four boxes — who builds, who uses, who pays or carries the cost — and what flows along each arrow between them (Lab 04, Box 3). (Lesson 1)
Innovation triangle
Desirability (Design Thinking), viability (Lean Startup, Business Model Canvas), feasibility and delivery (Scrum, Kanban). (Lesson 1)
Team execution
Turning a goal into a usable result, again and again, under uncertainty. (Lesson 2)
3E's
This course's working framing of team execution — Empower, Experiment, Evolve; not a published framework. (Lesson 2)
E sentence test
A sentence passes only if it names an action the team takes and carries a date, an event or an owner. (Lesson 2)
Cross-functional
The members have all the skills necessary to create value each Sprint (Scrum Guide 2020). (Lesson 2)
Self-managing
The team internally decides who does what, when and how (Scrum Guide 2020). (Lesson 2)
Empowered team / feature team
Empowered: receives a problem to solve and is accountable for the result. Feature team: receives features whose solutions are already decided (Cagan). (Lesson 2)
Stream-aligned team
A team aligned to a flow of work from a segment of the business, owning it end to end (Team Topologies). (Lesson 2)
Type 1 / Type 2 decision
Type 1: consequential and irreversible or nearly so — decide slowly. Type 2: changeable and reversible — decide quickly, in small groups (Bezos, 2015 letter). (Lesson 2)
Experiment (five parts)
A question, a prediction written before, a countable measure, a time limit, a decision rule fixed in advance. (Lesson 2)
Evolve
Inspection and adaptation on a rhythm: the same numbers at every Sprint Review, exactly one written policy changed per Retrospective. (Lesson 2)
Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally
The sixth general practice of the Kanban Method (Kanban University). (Lesson 2)
Product discovery
Finding out what is worth building and whether users want it; its output is validated backlog items. (Lesson 2)
Product delivery
Building it well; its output is releasable product. (Lesson 2)
Continuous discovery and delivery
Both kinds of work running in parallel, by the same team, all the time (Cagan's later name for dual-track Agile). (Lesson 2)
Four product questions
Is it valuable, usable, feasible and viable? (Cagan) (Lesson 2)
Discovery WIP limit
This course's suggestion: one discovery card in progress at a time, next to the delivery cards. (Lesson 2)
Lean Startup
Eric Ries's method (2011) for creating new products under uncertainty through experiments; its unit of progress is validated learning. (Lesson 3)
Build–Measure–Learn
Build the smallest testable product, measure what real users do, learn whether to persevere or pivot — and go round the loop as fast as possible. (Lesson 3)
Validated learning
A belief about users confirmed or refuted by data from real users. (Lesson 3)
Riskiest assumption
The belief that, if false, makes the product pointless however well it is built; usually about users' behaviour. (Lesson 3)
MVP
The version of a new product that allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort (Ries). (Lesson 3)
Hypothesis (course template)
We believe that [segment] will [behaviour] because [reason]. We will know we are right when we see [metric] reach [number] by [date]. (Lesson 3)
Actionable metric
A count that shows cause and effect clearly enough to support a decision. (Lesson 3)
Vanity metric
A number that grows and looks good but supports no decision — for example, total visits since launch. (Lesson 3)
Decision rule
The number that means “persevere”, and the pivot to consider otherwise, written before the test. (Lesson 3)
Persevere
Keep the strategy after the hypothesis held, and build the next increment. (Lesson 3)
Pivot
A structured change of strategy that tests a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, the strategy or growth; the vision stays (Ries). (Lesson 3)
Pivot types (examples)
Customer segment, channel, customer need, zoom-in (Ries, 2011). (Lesson 3)
Business Model Canvas
Nine blocks that show how a business creates, delivers and captures value on one page (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010; Strategyzer). (Lesson 3)
Value Proposition
The gain a customer segment receives; the centre of the canvas. (Lesson 3)
Value Proposition Canvas
Strategyzer's zoom into one segment and its value proposition: jobs, pains and gains against products, pain relievers and gain creators. (Lesson 3)
Canvas v0 / v1
v0: the five customer-side blocks (today). v1: all nine blocks (Studio 2, December 7). (Lesson 3)
Welcome changing requirements
Manifesto principle 2: change, even late, used for the customer's advantage. (Lesson 4)
Technical excellence and good design
Manifesto principle 9: continuous attention to them enhances agility. (Lesson 4)
Adaptation
Changing the plan and the product from what inspection reveals (Scrum's third pillar). (Lesson 4)
Change without chaos
One entry door (the Product Backlog), a stable Sprint Goal, and a design that keeps typical changes small. (Lesson 4)
Technical debt
The extra work every future change costs because the code reflects an earlier understanding or a shortcut (Cunningham, 1992). (Lesson 4)
Interest / refactoring
Interest: the extra work on each later change. Refactoring: rewriting the design to match the current understanding, which removes the interest. (Lesson 4)
Technical debt quadrant
Deliberate or inadvertent × prudent or reckless — four kinds of technical debt (Fowler, 2009). (Lesson 4)
Component
A named part of the product with one responsibility. (Lesson 4)
Interface
The agreed way in which a component exchanges information with the others. (Lesson 4)
Information hiding
Each module hides one design decision that is likely to change, so a change touches one module only (Parnas, 1972). (Lesson 4)
API
An interface for programs: the requests a component accepts and the responses it returns. (Lesson 4)
API-first
Design and agree the interface before the implementation; the opposite is code-first (OpenAPI Initiative). (Lesson 4)
Monolith / microservices
One deployable unit / many small, independently deployable services that communicate through interfaces. (Lesson 4)
Monolith First
Start with one well-structured unit and split it when the boundaries are clear (Fowler, 2015). (Lesson 4)
Loosely coupled architecture
Lets small teams make changes that can be deployed safely and independently (DORA). (Lesson 4)
Coupling and flow
Coupled design → large cards → long cycle times → more WIP → longer cycle and lead times for all (Little's Law). (Lesson 4)
