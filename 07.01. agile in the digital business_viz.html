1.1 · A method is a decision about the product

🎞 6

In Class 1 you met the word methodology: a shared way of working that sets how a team plans, decides, delivers and learns. The syllabus asks a further question this week: what is the link between a methodology and the organization, its products and its services? The answer of this lesson is literal, and you can check it with arithmetic. The way an organization arranges its work fixes how often its customers receive something new, how soon it learns whether that new thing is useful, and how much it costs to change direction. A department that releases a new version once a year can learn from its customers at most once a year. A team that releases a usable increment every two weeks can learn up to 26 times in the same year (52 weeks ÷ 2). The two organizations may employ equally capable people; they still end up with different products, because one of them was able to change its product from real feedback twenty-five more times.

🎞 7

Stephen Denning, the author of your course text, studied firms that work this way and summarized what they share as three laws (Strategy & Leadership, 2016). The Law of the Small Team: work is done by small, autonomous, cross-functional teams, in short cycles, on small tasks, with continuous feedback from users. The Law of the Customer: the purpose of the organization is to add value for customers, and everyone can see how their own work contributes to it. The Law of the Network: the organization operates as a network of teams that work toward a common goal, not as a top-down hierarchy.

🎞 8

Two of his cases show the effect on the product. Before 2011, Ericsson built its systems on a five-year cycle in units of several thousand people; after its move to Agile, Denning reports less work in progress and deployments one to two years earlier than before. At Spotify, the Discover Weekly feature was built by one of more than a hundred small teams; and Barclays, a bank more than three centuries old, made Agile a strategic initiative in 2015 (Denning, 2016). The companies differ in age and industry. What changed in each was the organization of work, and the product changed with it.

1.2 · Two rules about structure: Conway's Law and the two-pizza team

🎞 9

Two older observations explain why the organization and the product are so closely tied. In April 1968, Melvin Conway published the thesis now called Conway's Law: organizations that design systems produce designs that copy the communication structures of those organizations (Conway, 1968). In practice, if three departments that rarely talk to each other build one product, the product tends to have three parts that connect badly exactly where the departments do not talk. If one small team owns the whole product, the parts are designed together. Lesson 4 returns to this observation from the side of the system.

The second is a company policy. Amazon describes its two-pizza team rule this way: no team should be larger than can be fed with two pizzas — in practice fewer than about ten people; each team owns one product or service and its customers from start to finish; when the work grows, the team is split into new small teams instead of being enlarged (AWS Executive Insights). The reason is the number of communication paths. In a team of n people there are n(n−1)/2 pairs who may need to talk: 4 people have 6 pairs, 12 people have 66. The rule is Denning's Law of the Small Team written as an explicit company policy.

1.3 · Scrum's path to today's digital workplace

🎞 10

Scrum's history is the shortest version of this story. In 1986 Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka described, in the Harvard Business Review, product-development teams at Japanese manufacturers that worked in overlapping phases and organized themselves; they named the approach after the scrum formation in rugby. In 1995 Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland first co-presented Scrum at the OOPSLA (Object-Oriented Programming, Systems, Languages and Applications conference) conference; the Agile Manifesto followed in 2001 and the first Scrum Guide in 2010. The 2020 edition, the current one, was made “even less prescriptive” and removed “any remaining inference to IT (Information Technology) work” such as testing, system, design and requirement (Scrum Guide revisions). The Guide itself notes that Scrum is now used well beyond software product development, where it has its roots (Scrum Guide 2020). Scrum outside IT is therefore not a local variation: it is what the authors wrote.

🎞 11

Around Scrum, three practices define what the syllabus calls the digital workplace. Continuous Integration (CI): every member integrates their work into a shared repository at least daily, and an automated build with tests checks every integration (Fowler). Continuous Delivery (CD): the ability to get changes of all types — features, fixes, experiments — to users quickly, safely and in a sustainable way, so that the product is always releasable and a release becomes a business decision rather than a technical event (Humble). DevOps (Development and Operations): the people who build the product and the people who operate it work as one team, with shared responsibility for the product in use. None of the three is required by the Scrum Guide; all three exist because a two-week increment is useful only if it can reach users. The Guide also prescribes no location: it requires that the events take place and that the artifacts are visible to every member, which is why distributed teams can run Scrum.

Industry measures delivery with the research programme DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment). Its software delivery measures include change lead time, “the amount of time it takes for a change to go from committed to version control to deployed in production”, and deployment frequency, the number of deployments over a given period (DORA, 2026). Change lead time is last week's lead time applied to one change: the measurement you computed on your own cards is the one researchers use to compare delivery performance between organizations.

1.4 · Product, service, and the value map

🎞 12

In a digital business the product and the service cannot be separated. The product is what the user receives — for Team Menu, the allergen page. The service is the continuing activity that keeps value reaching the user — someone enters the day's menu before 11:00, someone answers “wrong entry?” messages within a day. A page that nobody updates shows yesterday's allergens, which is worse than no page at all. A method that delivers features but leaves nobody responsible for the daily service fails the Law of the Customer, however well its Sprints run.

To see both at once, this course uses a one-quarter-page value map with four boxes: who builds the product (the team, and anyone whose data or work it depends on), who uses it, who pays for it or carries its cost, and what flows along each arrow between them — data, money, time, attention, or a legal duty met. An empty box is a finding, not a failure: you write “unknown — to ask whom, by when”. The value map is Box 3 of Lab 04.

1.5 · The innovation triangle

🎞 13

The last idea of this lesson is a map of the frameworks themselves. IDEO describes design thinking as the search for solutions that are at the same time desirable, feasible and viable (IDEO). This course draws the three criteria as a triangle and puts on each corner the framework whose main question it is:

  • Desirability — do people want it, and will they use it? The question of Design Thinking (Classes 10 to 13, Lab 05).
  • Viability — can it sustain itself: who pays, and does the business model hold? The question of Lean Startup and the Business Model Canvas (Lesson 3 today; the canvas v1 in Studio 2).
  • Feasibility — can we build it, and how do we deliver it in increments? The question of Scrum and Kanban (Classes 3 to 6).

The corners touch. Team Menu's test in Lesson 3 measures a behaviour of students — a desirability fact — on which its business model depends — a viability question. That is normal: the triangle tells you which framework to reach for first, not which questions are allowed. Activity 1 asks your team to place its biggest open question on one corner.

2.1 · Team execution: three conditions, and two tests
🎞 18
The syllabus speaks of “team execution when creating new products and services”. Team execution means turning a goal into a usable result, again and again, under uncertainty. When you read Denning's cases and the Scrum Guide side by side, three conditions appear wherever teams execute well: the team is allowed to decide how to reach its goal; the team can try ideas cheaply before it commits to them; and the team learns on a fixed rhythm. This course names the three conditions the 3E's (Empower, Experiment, Evolve) — Empower, Experiment, Evolve. The 3E's are a working framing of this course, not a published framework: the syllabus names them without a definition, and this is the definition you are examined on. Each E rests on a documented source, given below.
🎞 19
Two tests turn the 3E's (Empower, Experiment, Evolve) from words into practice. An E sentence passes only if (1) it names an action the team takes — a decision, a test, a rhythm — and (2) it carries a date, an event or an owner. “We empower everyone” fails both tests. “The PO (Product Owner) orders the backlog alone after hearing the team, at the Daily Scrum” passes both.
2.2 · Empower — a goal, and the authority to reach it
🎞 20
The Scrum Guide 2020 describes the team with two words. Scrum Teams are cross-functional: “the members have all the skills necessary to create value each Sprint”. They are also self-managing: “they internally decide who does what, when, and how” (Scrum Guide 2020). Marty Cagan, of the Silicon Valley Product Group, adds the distinction that product organizations use: an empowered product team receives a problem to solve and is accountable for the result, while a feature team receives a list of features whose solutions someone else has already decided. Amazon's two-pizza teams work under single-threaded leaders who set direction and remove obstacles instead of approving each decision (AWS). Team Topologies calls a team that owns a product from end to end a stream-aligned team, “aligned to a flow of work from (usually) a segment of the business domain” (Skelton & Pais).
Empowerment is not the absence of direction. The Product Goal and the Sprint Goal say what the team is trying to achieve; empowerment concerns how, and who does it. In a student team it is concrete: the PO (Product Owner) orders the Product Backlog alone after hearing everyone, the Developers decide how to build and who pairs with whom, and nobody waits for the professor's permission to try a way of working. The common failure is the opposite: a team that receives tasks one by one and must ask before each step is a feature team, however Agile its vocabulary.
2.3 · Experiment — small, cheap, reversible tests
🎞 21
Jeff Bezos's letter to Amazon shareholders for 2015 separates two kinds of decisions (2015 Letter to Shareholders). Type 1 decisions are “consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible”: they must be made slowly and carefully. Most decisions are Type 2 — “changeable, reversible” — and can be made quickly by individuals or small groups. The letter warns that large organizations tend to use the slow Type 1 process for Type 2 decisions, and then become slow and reluctant to experiment.
🎞 22
An experiment, in this course, turns an uncertain question into a Type 2 decision. Instead of building an app for a semester and hoping, a team tapes a sign with a link to a counter for four days and counts how many people use it; instead of arguing for a week about a technical choice, a team spends one hour on a spike. Every experiment has five parts: a question; a prediction written before the test; a measure that can be counted; a time limit; and a decision rule fixed in advance — what the team will do if the prediction holds, and what if it does not. Lesson 3 gives the full form of the prediction, the hypothesis. Size matters: an experiment that costs a month is no longer cheap, and one whose result cannot change any decision is not worth running.
2.4 · Evolve — inspection and adaptation on a rhythm
🎞 23
You met the source of the third E in Class 3: Scrum's pillars of inspection and adaptation, and the Sprint Retrospective. Kanban says the same in the sixth general practice of the Kanban Method, “improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally” (Kanban University). Evolve, in this course, means that improvement is a habit with a rhythm, not a mood. At every Sprint Review the team reads the same numbers — velocity, throughput, cycle time, the age of the open cards — and compares them with the previous Sprint. At every Retrospective it changes exactly one written policy, with an owner and a date on which the change will be checked.
One change per Retrospective is a rule of method, not of modesty. A team that changes five things at once cannot tell which change helped, and it cannot undo the one that did harm.
2.5 · Discovery and delivery in one team
🎞 24
A team that builds a new product does two kinds of work. Product discovery finds out what is worth building and whether users want it. Product delivery builds it well. Marty Cagan put the distinction in one sentence each: the discovery track “is all about quickly generating validated product backlog items”, and the delivery track “is all about generating releasable software” (Cagan, 2012). The two run in parallel, not as phases: discovery for the next items happens while the current items are delivered, by the same people. Cagan later stopped using the name “dual-track Agile” and speaks of continuous discovery and continuous delivery, to stress that neither ever stops (SVPG).
Discovery answers four questions about every idea, which Cagan lists in the second video of this lesson: is it valuable (will users choose it), usable (can they work out how), feasible (can we build it with our skills and time), and viable (does it work for the business)? Three of those four words are corners of the innovation triangle.
🎞 25
In Scrum terms, both kinds of work are visible on the same board. Discovery cards — an interview, an MVP (Minimum Viable Product) test, a spike, a question to a stakeholder — sit next to delivery cards, the user stories; the Sprint Review is where discovery's evidence changes the order of the Product Backlog. This course suggests one practical rule for student teams: a separate WIP (Work In Progress) limit of one for discovery cards, so that discovery always happens and never takes over the Sprint. The share of discovery is not fixed: a new product, like yours, needs more of it than a mature one.

3.1 · Build–Measure–Learn
🎞 30
Lesson 2 said that an experiment turns an uncertain question into a small, reversible decision. Lean Startup is the method that makes experiments the main activity of a team that creates something new. Eric Ries described it in The Lean Startup (2011); his principles page states that startups exist to learn how to build a sustainable business, and that this learning “can be validated scientifically, by running experiments that allow us to test each element of our vision” (theleanstartup.com). His first principle is that entrepreneurs are everywhere: the method is meant for any team that creates a new product under uncertainty — a company team, a public service, a student team.
The method runs in a loop of three steps. Build: turn an idea into the smallest product that can test it. Measure: observe how real users respond — what they do, not what they say they would do. Learn: decide whether to persevere or to pivot. Ries asks that every process of a new venture be “geared to accelerate that feedback loop”: the faster a team goes round it, the more it learns per month. The unit of progress is validated learning — a belief about users confirmed or refuted by data from real users — not lines of code or features built.
🎞 31
Lean Startup and Scrum combine without conflict. Scrum gives the rhythm: the Sprint is the time-box, the MVP (Minimum Viable Product) test is a discovery card, and the Sprint Review is the moment of the Learn step, with the stakeholders in the room. Lean Startup gives the question each rhythm must answer. A team that runs Sprints without a hypothesis delivers increments; a team that runs Sprints with one also learns whether those increments are worth delivering.
3.2 · From the riskiest assumption to a hypothesis that can fail
🎞 32
The loop starts with the belief to test. The riskiest assumption is the belief that, if it is false, makes the product pointless however well it is built; it is usually about the users' behaviour, not about the technology. “Students will check a web page before they line up” is riskier than “we can build a web page”: the second is almost certainly true, and nobody has checked the first.
The MVP (Minimum Viable Product), in Ries's definition, is “that version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort”. An MVP is defined by the question it answers, not by its polish. A printed sign with a QR (Quick Response) code linking to a page updated by hand, a shared document sent by e-mail, a person doing by hand what software would do later — all of these have served as MVPs (Minimum Viable Products). If the MVP needs more than a day or two to build, it is probably not minimal yet.
🎞 33
The belief becomes testable when it is written as a hypothesis. This course uses a template extended from the one Jeff Gothelf and Josh Seiden published in Lean UX (User Experience) (Gothelf):
We believe that [these people] will [do this] because [reason]. We will know we are right when we see [metric] reach [number] by [date].
Six parts: segment, behaviour, reason, metric, number, date. Without the number and the date the sentence cannot fail, so it cannot teach anything.
🎞 34
The metric must be something you can count without asking anyone's name: opens of a link, sign-ups, returning visitors, minutes saved. Ries separates actionable metrics, which show cause and effect clearly enough to support a decision, from vanity metrics, which grow and look good but support no decision — “total visits since September” rises whatever you do. Finally, the decision rule is written before the test: which number means “persevere”, and which pivot you will consider if it is not reached. A rule written after the result will always find a way to call the result a success.
3.3 · Persevere or pivot
🎞 35
When the test ends, the team compares the measured number with the rule. If the hypothesis held, it perseveres: it keeps the strategy and builds the next increment. If not, it considers a pivot, which Ries defines as a structured change of strategy designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, the strategy or the way the business grows. A pivot keeps the vision and changes one element. Ries's book lists several types, among them the customer segment pivot (the product works, but for a different group of people than planned), the channel pivot (the same product, reached in a different way), the customer need pivot (the users have a more important problem than the one you solve) and the zoom-in pivot (one feature becomes the whole product). A pivot is neither a failure nor a restart: it is the next experiment, chosen with what the last one taught.
Two rules keep the decision honest. The rule is fixed before the test, as in 3.2. And the people who decide are the whole team, at the Sprint Review, with the stakeholders' observations in the room — not one member reading the numbers alone on a Sunday night.
3.4 · The Business Model Canvas
🎞 36

4.1 · Change without chaos
🎞 43
The second principle behind the Agile Manifesto reads: “Welcome changing requirements, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage.” The ninth reads: “Continuous attention to technical excellence and good design enhances agility” (agilemanifesto.org). Read together, they say that welcoming change is a property of the product's design as much as an attitude of the team. Adaptation — Scrum's third pillar — means changing the plan and the product from what inspection reveals; the question of this lesson is what that change costs.
Change without chaos needs three conditions. First, change enters through one door: the Product Backlog, ordered by the PO (Product Owner) — not through side conversations with one developer. Second, the Sprint Goal stays stable: the Scrum Guide allows the scope of a Sprint to be clarified and renegotiated with the PO, but no change may endanger the Sprint Goal (Scrum Guide 2020). Third, the design of the product keeps the cost of a typical change small. You practised the first two in Classes 4 and 5. The third is the subject of the next three sections, and it is the one CS (Computer Science) students can influence most directly.
4.2 · Technical debt
🎞 44
In 1992 Ward Cunningham wrote an experience report about WyCash, a financial software system his team had built, and introduced a term that every software team now uses (Cunningham, 1992). Technical debt, in the sense of that report, is the extra work that every future change costs because the code still reflects an earlier, simpler understanding of the problem, or a shortcut taken to deliver sooner. The extra work on each later change is usually called the interest; rewriting the design so that it matches the current understanding — refactoring — removes it. In his 2009 video, the first of this lesson, Cunningham insists on one point: the term was never meant as permission to write careless code; it describes code that was reasonable when it was written and that must be revised as the team learns.
🎞 45
Martin Fowler sorts technical debt along two questions — did the team know it was taking the shortcut, and was the shortcut sensible? — which gives four kinds (Fowler, 2009). Prudent and deliberate: “we ship now and correct it after the test”, with the correction planned. Reckless and deliberate: “no time for tests”. Prudent and inadvertent: “now that we know the product better, we see how it should have been designed”. Reckless and inadvertent: a team that does not know basic design practices and does not notice the cost it is creating.
Technical debt can be measured in the units of Class 6: count the extra days it adds to the cards that touch it. Team Menu's debt from Lab 03 was prudent and deliberate — the dish names were typed directly into the page, to have something for the first review — and its interest was visible on every menu change: two pages to edit, English and French, and one more check. The team corrected it on October 30.
4.3 · Components, interfaces, APIs
🎞 46
The design idea that keeps changes small is old and precise. A component is a named part of a product with one responsibility; an interface is the agreed way in which a component exchanges information with the others. In 1972 David Parnas showed that the way a system is divided into modules decides how expensive later changes are, and proposed the criterion now called information hiding: each module should hide one design decision that is likely to change, so that when the decision changes, only that module changes (Parnas, 1972). A team that cannot name its components cannot tell which of them a request touches.
🎞 47
An API (Application Programming Interface) is an interface for programs: a defined set of requests a component accepts and responses it returns. When a team designs and agrees the interface before it writes the implementation, it works API-first; the OpenAPI Initiative contrasts this with code-first work, where the implementation comes first and the description of the interface is generated afterwards (OpenAPI Initiative). The OpenAPI Specification is an open standard for describing an API (Application Programming Interface) in a JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) or YAML (YAML Ain't Markup Language, a data format) document. The benefit of API-first is organizational as much as technical: two people — or two teams — can build the two sides at the same time, and each side can later change its inside without asking the other.
How many deployable parts should a product have? A monolith is one deployable unit; microservices are many small services, each deployable on its own, that communicate through interfaces (Lewis & Fowler, 2014). For a new product, Fowler's advice is Monolith First: start with one well-structured unit and split it only when experience has made the boundaries between parts clear (Fowler, 2015). For your product the point is not the number of units; it is that you can name three to five components inside it and say which one a change touches. DORA (DevOps Research and Assessment)'s research describes the payoff: a loosely coupled architecture “lets small and productive teams make changes that can be deployed safely and independently” (DORA). And Conway's Law, from Lesson 1, works in both directions: the components mirror the conversations, so a clear interface is also a clear agreement between people.
4.4 · How a design decision changes WIP and lead times
🎞 48
Now connect the design to the numbers of Class 6. In a tightly coupled design, a typical change touches many components, so it becomes one large card. A large card has a long cycle time. While it waits for a review or for an answer, people start other cards, so WIP (Work In Progress) rises — and Little's Law, average cycle time = average WIP ÷ throughput, says that at the same throughput a higher WIP means longer cycle times for everything. A large card also occupies one WIP slot for many days, so the cards behind it wait and their lead times grow. In a loosely coupled design the same request touches one or two components, becomes one or two small cards, fits under the WIP (Work In Progress) limit, and comes out in days.

Terms to learn — all four lessons
Methodology
A shared way of working that sets how a team plans, decides, delivers and learns (Class 1). (Lesson 1)
Feedback cycles per year
How often an organization can learn from users in one year: 52 weeks ÷ weeks per release — 26 for two-week increments, 1 for a yearly release. (Lesson 1)
Law of the Small Team
Work is done by small, autonomous, cross-functional teams in short cycles, on small tasks, with continuous user feedback (Denning). (Lesson 1)
Law of the Customer
The purpose of the organization is to add value for customers; everyone can see how their own work contributes (Denning). (Lesson 1)
Law of the Network
The organization works as a network of teams toward a common goal, not as a top-down hierarchy (Denning). (Lesson 1)
Conway's Law (1968)
Organizations that design systems produce designs that copy their own communication structures. (Lesson 1)
Two-pizza team rule
Amazon's policy: teams small enough to be fed by two pizzas (under about ten people), each owning one product or service end to end; split them, do not enlarge them. (Lesson 1)
Communication paths
n(n−1)/2 possible pairs in a team of n people: 6 for 4 people, 66 for 12. (Lesson 1)
CI
Integrating every member's work into a shared repository at least daily, checked by an automated build and tests. (Lesson 1)
CD
The ability to get changes of all types to users quickly, safely and sustainably; the product is always releasable. (Lesson 1)
DevOps
The builders and the operators of a product working as one team with shared responsibility for the product in use. (Lesson 1)
Change lead time (DORA)
The time a change takes from being committed to version control to being deployed in production. (Lesson 1)
Product / service
The product is what the user receives; the service is the continuing activity that keeps value reaching the user. (Lesson 1)
Value map
Four boxes — who builds, who uses, who pays or carries the cost — and what flows along each arrow between them (Lab 04, Box 3). (Lesson 1)
Innovation triangle
Desirability (Design Thinking), viability (Lean Startup, Business Model Canvas), feasibility and delivery (Scrum, Kanban). (Lesson 1)
Team execution
Turning a goal into a usable result, again and again, under uncertainty. (Lesson 2)
3E's
This course's working framing of team execution — Empower, Experiment, Evolve; not a published framework. (Lesson 2)
E sentence test
A sentence passes only if it names an action the team takes and carries a date, an event or an owner. (Lesson 2)
Cross-functional
The members have all the skills necessary to create value each Sprint (Scrum Guide 2020). (Lesson 2)
Self-managing
The team internally decides who does what, when and how (Scrum Guide 2020). (Lesson 2)
Empowered team / feature team
Empowered: receives a problem to solve and is accountable for the result. Feature team: receives features whose solutions are already decided (Cagan). (Lesson 2)
Stream-aligned team
A team aligned to a flow of work from a segment of the business, owning it end to end (Team Topologies). (Lesson 2)
Type 1 / Type 2 decision
Type 1: consequential and irreversible or nearly so — decide slowly. Type 2: changeable and reversible — decide quickly, in small groups (Bezos, 2015 letter). (Lesson 2)
Experiment (five parts)
A question, a prediction written before, a countable measure, a time limit, a decision rule fixed in advance. (Lesson 2)
Evolve
Inspection and adaptation on a rhythm: the same numbers at every Sprint Review, exactly one written policy changed per Retrospective. (Lesson 2)
Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally
The sixth general practice of the Kanban Method (Kanban University). (Lesson 2)
Product discovery
Finding out what is worth building and whether users want it; its output is validated backlog items. (Lesson 2)
Product delivery
Building it well; its output is releasable product. (Lesson 2)
Continuous discovery and delivery
Both kinds of work running in parallel, by the same team, all the time (Cagan's later name for dual-track Agile). (Lesson 2)
Four product questions
Is it valuable, usable, feasible and viable? (Cagan) (Lesson 2)
Discovery WIP limit
This course's suggestion: one discovery card in progress at a time, next to the delivery cards. (Lesson 2)
Lean Startup
Eric Ries's method (2011) for creating new products under uncertainty through experiments; its unit of progress is validated learning. (Lesson 3)
Build–Measure–Learn
Build the smallest testable product, measure what real users do, learn whether to persevere or pivot — and go round the loop as fast as possible. (Lesson 3)
Validated learning
A belief about users confirmed or refuted by data from real users. (Lesson 3)
Riskiest assumption
The belief that, if false, makes the product pointless however well it is built; usually about users' behaviour. (Lesson 3)
MVP
The version of a new product that allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort (Ries). (Lesson 3)
Hypothesis (course template)
We believe that [segment] will [behaviour] because [reason]. We will know we are right when we see [metric] reach [number] by [date]. (Lesson 3)
Actionable metric
A count that shows cause and effect clearly enough to support a decision. (Lesson 3)
Vanity metric
A number that grows and looks good but supports no decision — for example, total visits since launch. (Lesson 3)
Decision rule
The number that means “persevere”, and the pivot to consider otherwise, written before the test. (Lesson 3)
Persevere
Keep the strategy after the hypothesis held, and build the next increment. (Lesson 3)
Pivot
A structured change of strategy that tests a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, the strategy or growth; the vision stays (Ries). (Lesson 3)
Pivot types (examples)
Customer segment, channel, customer need, zoom-in (Ries, 2011). (Lesson 3)
Business Model Canvas
Nine blocks that show how a business creates, delivers and captures value on one page (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010; Strategyzer). (Lesson 3)
Value Proposition
The gain a customer segment receives; the centre of the canvas. (Lesson 3)
Value Proposition Canvas
Strategyzer's zoom into one segment and its value proposition: jobs, pains and gains against products, pain relievers and gain creators. (Lesson 3)
Canvas v0 / v1
v0: the five customer-side blocks (today). v1: all nine blocks (Studio 2, December 7). (Lesson 3)
Welcome changing requirements
Manifesto principle 2: change, even late, used for the customer's advantage. (Lesson 4)
Technical excellence and good design
Manifesto principle 9: continuous attention to them enhances agility. (Lesson 4)
Adaptation
Changing the plan and the product from what inspection reveals (Scrum's third pillar). (Lesson 4)
Change without chaos
One entry door (the Product Backlog), a stable Sprint Goal, and a design that keeps typical changes small. (Lesson 4)
Technical debt
The extra work every future change costs because the code reflects an earlier understanding or a shortcut (Cunningham, 1992). (Lesson 4)
Interest / refactoring
Interest: the extra work on each later change. Refactoring: rewriting the design to match the current understanding, which removes the interest. (Lesson 4)
Technical debt quadrant
Deliberate or inadvertent × prudent or reckless — four kinds of technical debt (Fowler, 2009). (Lesson 4)
Component
A named part of the product with one responsibility. (Lesson 4)
Interface
The agreed way in which a component exchanges information with the others. (Lesson 4)
Information hiding
Each module hides one design decision that is likely to change, so a change touches one module only (Parnas, 1972). (Lesson 4)
API
An interface for programs: the requests a component accepts and the responses it returns. (Lesson 4)
API-first
Design and agree the interface before the implementation; the opposite is code-first (OpenAPI Initiative). (Lesson 4)
Monolith / microservices
One deployable unit / many small, independently deployable services that communicate through interfaces. (Lesson 4)
Monolith First
Start with one well-structured unit and split it when the boundaries are clear (Fowler, 2015). (Lesson 4)
Loosely coupled architecture
Lets small teams make changes that can be deployed safely and independently (DORA). (Lesson 4)
Coupling and flow
Coupled design → large cards → long cycle times → more WIP → longer cycle and lead times for all (Little's Law). (Lesson 4)
