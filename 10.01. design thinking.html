Lesson 1 · What Design Thinking is, where it comes from, and how it fits next to Agile

▶ Slide 5

1.1 A working definition

▶ Slides 6–7

Design Thinking is a human-centred approach to problems that are not yet well understood. IDEO, the design firm most associated with the term, describes it as an approach that integrates three things: the needs of people, the possibilities of technology and the requirements for business success. In practice it is a set of activities — observing and interviewing users, framing the problem, generating many ideas, building cheap prototypes and testing them with users — that a team repeats until the evidence shows that a solution works for the people it is meant for.

The three requirements are usually called the three lenses of innovation: desirability (do people want it and will they use it?), feasibility (can we build it with the technology and skills we have?) and viability (can the organisation sustain it — costs, rules, business model?). Design Thinking deliberately starts with desirability, but a concept is ready only when it passes all three.

IDEO itself states that there is no single official definition (IDEO FAQ). This course uses the definition above together with the five modes of the Stanford d.school, because both are free, documented and widely taught.

1.2 Where the method comes from

▶ Slides 8–9
  • 1969 — Herbert A. Simon, in The Sciences of the Artificial, treats design as a discipline of its own: devising courses of action that change an existing situation into a preferred one. In this sense engineers, managers and doctors all design.
  • 1980s–1990s — human-centred design spreads in ergonomics and software: products are designed around observed users and evaluated with them, not only against technical specifications. Today the idea is an international standard, ISO 9241-210:2019.
  • 1991 — IDEO is founded in Palo Alto. Its later CEO Tim Brown popularises the term design thinking in a HBR (Harvard Business Review) article (2008) and in the book Change by Design (2009).
  • 2004 — the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford, the d.school, starts teaching the method to students from every faculty. Its free Design Thinking Bootleg describes five modes: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test.
  • 2004 — the UK Design Council publishes the Double Diamond, developed in 2003, with four phases: Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver. In 2019 it becomes the Framework for Innovation and adds four principles: be people-centred, communicate visually and inclusively, collaborate and co-create, iterate.

1.3 Five modes, two diamonds

▶ Slide 10

The d.school calls its five steps modes, not stages, because a team moves between them as evidence arrives. The table shows the question each mode answers and where it sits on the Design Council's Double Diamond.

Mode (d.school)Question the team answersTypical outputDouble Diamond phase
EmpathizeWho are the users, what do they do, what do they need?interview notes, observationsDiscover — diverge
DefineWhich problem, for whom, is worth solving?empathy map, POV (Point of View), HMW (How Might We) questionsDefine — converge
IdeateWhich different ways could solve it?many ideas, a shortlistDevelop — diverge
PrototypeWhat is the cheapest thing we can build to learn?paper screens, a mock-upDevelop → Deliver
TestDoes it work for the users? What did we learn?observed problems, decisionsDeliver — converge

Each diamond has two halves. In the first half the team diverges: it widens the set of options — more observations, more ideas — and postpones judgment. In the second half it converges: it compares, groups and chooses. The first diamond is about finding the right problem; the second is about solving that problem well. Generating and judging are kept apart in time because criticism during generation narrows the options too early; this is the reason for the brainstorming rule “defer judgment” in Lesson 3.

1.4 Iterative, not linear

▶ Slide 11

The five modes are not a checklist completed once. A test often shows that the problem was framed badly (back to Define) or that an idea needs a different variant (back to Ideate). The d.school's advice is to build prototypes with conviction but to test as if you know you're wrong. A loop is the expected result of testing, not a failure of the team.

Team Menu plans at least one loop: the Studio 3 test on December 14 leaves enough time to fix one serious problem and retest before the December 20 deadline.

1.5 Design Thinking, Lean Startup and Scrum: the innovation triangle

▶ Slide 12
MethodMain questionMain unit of workWhere you met it in DIG 123
Design ThinkingAre we solving the right problem for the right people?insight, prototypetoday, Lab 05 and the Studio Weeks
Lean StartupIs our business assumption true?MVP (Minimum Viable Product) experiment with a pass/fail metricLab 04 — QR (Quick Response)-sign test, Nov 3–6
Agile / ScrumHow do we deliver value in small, inspected increments?Sprint, IncrementSprints 1–3, Labs 02–04

The three methods answer different questions, so organisations combine them instead of choosing one. A frequent sequence is: Design Thinking finds a desirable problem and a concept; Lean Startup turns the riskiest business assumption into a measurable experiment; Scrum builds the product in Sprints and inspects it at every Sprint Review. Insights from interviews become Product Backlog items, which keeps the Product Owner's ordering grounded in evidence about users.

Lesson 2 · Empathize and Define

▶ Slide 17

2.1 Empathize: evidence from people, not opinions about them

▶ Slide 18

In the Empathize mode the team collects first-hand evidence about the people it designs for: what they do, what they say, what they find difficult and in which context. The three basic techniques are observation (watching the real situation — the cafeteria line at 12:30), interviews (one person, one interviewer, one note-taker) and immersion (doing the task yourself — finding the allergens of today's pasta in under a minute).

The output is raw evidence: quotations, observed actions and the context in which they happened. It is not yet a conclusion. A common beginner mistake is to interview people about the solution the team already likes; that produces polite approval, not information.

2.2 Interview questions that produce evidence

▶ Slides 19–20

Good interview questions ask about concrete past behaviour (“Tell me about the last time…”), because people describe what they did more accurately than they predict what they would do. Four question types matter:

TypeExampleUse it?
Open“Tell me about the last time you chose a hot meal here.”Yes — the core of the guide.
Closed“Do you read the menu board?”Rarely — rewrite it: “How do you use the menu board?”
Leading“Wouldn't a ‘safe for me’ filter be great?”No — it suggests the answer.
Hypothetical“Would you use an allergen page if we built one?”Not in Empathize — keep it for Test, when people can try a prototype.

Follow-up questions such as “Why?”, “What happened next?” or “Can you show me?” — called probing — turn a short answer into a story. Silence also works: wait three seconds before asking the next question.

How many people? For one fairly similar group, five interviews usually reveal the main patterns. Team Menu plans five real interviews with eight open questions and a ten-minute limit each.

2.3 Consent and privacy: what we will not collect

▶ Slide 21

Information about allergies and intolerances is health data. The EU (European Union)'s GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) treats health data as a special category (Article 9) and requires that only data necessary for the purpose is collected — data minimisation, Article 5(1)(c). For a student project the safe rule is short: ask how people choose and check food, never about their medical situation.

  • Adults only. Read the three-line consent script before the first question and stop if the person hesitates.
  • No names, no contact details, no recordings, no photos of people.
  • Do not ask why someone avoids an ingredient; if they tell you, do not write it down.
  • Notes contain paraphrased insights, not identities. Raw notes are deleted after December 20.
  • Your university's rules on research with people apply first; if unsure, ask in the Q&A (Questions and Answers) Parking Lot before interviewing.
Consent script (3 lines): (1) “We are students in an Agile course testing ideas for a cafeteria allergen page; this takes about ten minutes.” (2) “We will not record you or write down your name, and you can skip any question or stop at any time.” (3) “We will ask how you choose and check food, not about your health — is that all right for you?”

2.4 Making sense of the notes: the empathy map and the persona

▶ Slide 22

An empathy map is a four-part board that sorts research notes about one user, or one group of similar users, into Says (direct quotations), Thinks (beliefs and worries the person expressed), Does (observed actions) and Feels (emotions the person named or showed). NN/g (Nielsen Norman Group) recommends building it from real research rather than assumptions, and using it to spot contradictions — for example, a student who says the menu is clear but does ask the cook about every dish.

After mapping, the team groups related notes into themes and counts them. In Example 2, twelve notes from two practice interviews produce four themes: trust in the information (5 notes), time pressure (3), habit (2) and fear of being exposed in the queue (2).

A persona is a fictional profile that summarises a research-based user group, so the team keeps one kind of user in mind while it designs. Team Menu's persona is Inès, 20, a second-year hospitality student who avoids one ingredient. She is a composite of the interviews, not a real person, and her reason for avoiding the ingredient is not recorded.

2.5 Define: the Point of View and How Might We questions

▶ Slides 23–24

In the Define mode the team turns themes into one problem statement. The d.school format is a Point of View (POV (Point of View)): [user] needs [need] because [insight]. The need is written as a verb, not as a feature; the insight is something the team learned and did not know before.

POV — A student who must avoid an ingredient needs to be certain a dish is safe before she joins the line, because asking at the counter in front of others costs her time and makes her feel singled out.

The POV is then rewritten as How Might We (HMW (How Might We)) questions. “How” assumes that a solution exists, “might” allows ideas that may fail, “we” makes it a team task. The phrasing comes from Min Basadur's work at P&G (Procter & Gamble) in the 1970s and later spread through IDEO (NN/g (Nielsen Norman Group) on HMW questions). A useful HMW question is neither too broad (“How might we fix the cafeteria?”) nor so narrow that it already contains the solution (“How might we add a QR (Quick Response) code?”).

  1. How might we let her know a dish is safe before she reaches the counter?
  2. How might we make the time the menu was last checked visible?
  3. How might we let her ask a question without being seen asking?

  } else if (s.kind === "note") {

How the program works, step by step

  1. QUESTIONS holds the ten draft questions. RULES holds four tests in a fixed order — leading, hypothetical, closed, open — each made of a regular expression (a text pattern) and a reason.
  2. The order matters: “Wouldn't a … filter be great?” is tested for leading words before anything else, so it is labelled leading even though it is also a question about an idea.
  3. The fix step counts the labels with reduce, replaces the two closed questions with the rewrites in REWRITE, and keeps only the questions labelled open — eight remain.

  6. The pov and hmw steps write the POV (Point of View) and the three HMW (How Might We) questions in the result box; the execution window states which themes support each part.
  7. Limits: word rules are only a first check. “What would make you trust a label?” starts like an open question but asks for a prediction, so a person still reads every question.

3.1 Ideate: quantity first, judgment later

▶ Slide 30

In the Ideate mode the team produces many different answers to one HMW (How Might We) question, then selects a few to prototype. The quality of the final concept depends on the number and the variety of the options considered, which is why generating ideas and evaluating them are kept apart in time.

Alex F. Osborn, who introduced brainstorming in Applied Imagination (1953), proposed four rules: aim for quantity, withhold criticism, welcome unusual ideas, and combine and improve ideas. IDEO's seven rules extend them: defer judgment, encourage wild ideas, build on the ideas of others, stay focused on the topic, one conversation at a time, be visual, go for quantity.

Silent techniques such as brainwriting and Crazy 8s have one practical advantage over spoken brainstorming: everybody generates ideas at the same time, so nobody waits for a turn and the first or loudest speaker has less influence on the others. For a team of four, one round of Crazy 8s gives 4 × 8 = 32 sketches in eight minutes.

3.3 Converge: dot voting and the impact × feasibility grid

▶ Slide 32

To converge, each member first stars their own best sketches (a shortlist). Then the team uses dot voting: every member places a fixed number of dots — three in Team Menu — on the ideas they find strongest, silently and at the same time, so that nobody simply follows the first voter. Dots measure the team's preference, not proof; the evidence comes later, from testing.

Next the team scores each shortlisted idea from 1 to 5 for impact (how well it serves the POV (Point of View)) and feasibility (how easily the team can build and run it), and places it on a 2 × 2 grid: quick wins (high impact, high feasibility), big bets (high impact, low feasibility), fill-ins (low impact, high feasibility) and ideas to reconsider (low on both). Impact connects to the desirability lens and feasibility to the technology lens; viability is checked when the concept enters the BMC (Business Model Canvas) in Studio 2.

3.4 Prototype: build to learn

▶ Slide 33

A prototype is anything a user can see or handle that represents an idea well enough to produce feedback: a sketch, a sequence of paper screens, a cardboard model, a role-play of a service. Its purpose is learning, so it should be as cheap as the question allows.

4.1 Test: watch people use the prototype

▶ Slide 40

In a usability test a facilitator gives a tester realistic tasks and observes, without helping, while the tester tries to complete them. The tester is asked to think aloud — to say what they look at, expect and decide. NN/g (Nielsen Norman Group) calls thinking aloud the single most valuable usability method, because it shows not only that people struggle but why.

Tasks describe a goal, not the steps: “Find out whether today's pasta is safe for someone who avoids milk” — not “Tap the QR (Quick Response) code, then scroll to the milk icon”. Team Menu's Studio 3 test uses three tasks: (1) is the pasta of the day safe without milk? (2) when did the kitchen last check the menu? (3) report a wrong label.

4.2 How many testers? The five-user rule and its limits

▶ Slide 41

Jakob Nielsen and Thomas Landauer modelled the share of usability problems found by n testers as 1 − (1 − L)ⁿ, where L is the share of problems that one tester reveals; across their projects L averaged 31%. With five testers the formula gives about 85%, and every additional tester adds less. Their advice is therefore several small tests — five users, fix, test again — rather than one large test (NN/g (Nielsen Norman Group): Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users).

Two limits matter. The rule applies to qualitative tests with one fairly similar group of users; if users differ a lot — students and kitchen staff — test three or four people per group. And it does not apply to measuring numbers such as average task time, which needs much larger samples.

4.3 Rating what you found: severity × frequency

▶ Slide 42

Not every problem deserves the same attention. Nielsen's severity scale rates each problem from 0 to 4: 0 = not a usability problem, 1 = cosmetic, 2 = minor, 3 = major, 4 = catastrophe that must be fixed before release (NN/g (Nielsen Norman Group)). A rating combines how often the problem occurs, how much it hurts when it occurs and whether users can get past it. The mean of three people's ratings is more reliable than one person's.

Your studio sheets record only real problems, so you will use 1–4. To rank them, the course uses a simple studio rule: priority = severity × frequency, where frequency is the number of testers (out of five) who ran into the problem. In Example 4, “safe for me” read as “safe for everyone” scores 4 × 4 = 16 and is fixed first.

After the fix the team loops: Test → Ideate (new label variants) → Prototype (redraw two screens) → Test again with three new people.

4.5 Mindsets and limits

▶ Slide 45

The d.school lists mindsets that make the modes work: focus on human values, show don't tell, radical collaboration, bias toward action, embrace experimentation, be mindful of process, craft clarity. Three of them translate directly into your Studio work: bias toward action (prototype today instead of discussing for a week), show don't tell (bring the paper screens, not a description of them) and radical collaboration (every role contributes to every mode).

Design Thinking also has known weaknesses. Workshops can produce many sticky notes and no evidence from real users; five interviews can be misread as statistics; a persona can turn into a stereotype. The remedies are in this lecture: real users, quoted evidence, explicit severity ratings, numbers where numbers are needed (the Lab 04 counts) and a hand-over to Scrum, so that tested ideas become delivered increments.

  if (step >= STEPS.length) { pause(); return; }
  // Reads the step and moves on.

  if (s.k === "tester") {

    const hits = SHEET[s.i];

    const share = found.size / PROBLEMS.length;

    el("line", {x1:a[0], y1:a[1], x2:b[0], y2:b[1], stroke:"#0E7C7B", "stroke-width":3});
    // Dot at the new point.
    el("circle", {cx:b[0], cy:b[1], r:4, fill:"#0E7C7B"});

    const expect = 1 - Math.pow(1 - L, s.i + 1);
    // Logs what the tester showed and compares with the formula.
    addLog("Tester " + (s.i + 1) + " hits " + hits.join(", ") + " → found " + found.size + "/6 = " + Math.round(share * 100) + "% (formula predicts " + Math.round(expect * 100) + "%)");

How the program works, step by step

  1. PROBLEMS lists six problems with a severity agreed by three raters; SHEET lists, for each of the five testers, the problems that tester ran into.
  2. Each tester step draws a dot for every hit, increases freq[id] and adds the id to the set found; found.size / 6 is the observed share.
  3. drawBase() draws the curve 1 − (1 − L)ⁿ with L = 0.31 as a dashed line of 51 points; the observed share is drawn as solid segments, so the two can be compared directly.
  4. The priority step computes severity × frequency for every problem, sorts the list from high to low and draws one bar per problem.
  5. The five case steps fill the boxes Situation → Problem → Alternatives → Recommendation → Consequences for the top problem.
  6. Try it: change one row of SHEET (for example, let tester 3 also hit P6) and run again — the observed line reaches 100% and P6 gets a priority of 1.
